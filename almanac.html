Saturday 24 October — Ueno and Yanesen

Tokyo · a museum that calls itself the country's oldest, the country's only Le Corbusier building, and a district the war mostly missed
Tokyo National Museum東京国立博物館
Museum · Ueno · calls itself the oldest institution of its kind in Japan, dating its birth to 1872
▶
It began as an exhibition, not a building

The museum dates its own birth to an event. On 10 March 1872, the Ministry of Education's Museum Bureau opened an exhibition in the Taiseiden Hall of Yushima Seido — a former Confucian temple. It was scheduled for twenty days and extended by ten more because the crowds forced admission to be restricted. The catalogue listed over 600 items. Total attendance: 150,000 people, around 3,000 a day.

The museum's own summary: "This first exhibition in Japan organized by the government marked the birth of the Tokyo National Museum," and it "marked the inauguration of the nation's first museum with a permanent exhibit."

It moved to Ueno Park in 1882, with Emperor Meiji presiding over the opening of the main building and the zoo on 20 March that year.

⚑ The "oldest museum in Japan" line is the museum's own, and it carries a qualifier. Its 150th-anniversary page says "Tokyo National Museum (TNM) is the oldest institution of its kind in Japan," and the history pages qualify it differently again — "the nation's first museum with a permanent exhibit." ⇒ Two self-descriptions, both hedged; the flat claim is nobody's.

Tokyo National Museum official English history pages, quoted verbatim · checked 2026-08-15. The "oldest institution of its kind" wording is tnm.jp/150th/en/about.html · checked 2026-08-21

The buildings are the argument

Four buildings, four different answers to what a Japanese museum should look like:

The first main building was Western. Designed by the British architect Josiah Conder, built 1878–81, brick with two small domes. It was severely damaged in the Great Kanto Earthquake of 1923 and the museum ran on a single surviving building until 1938.

🔴 The Honkan that replaced it is deliberately not Western. Opened 10 November 1938, designed by Jin Watanabe after an open competition, and built in what is called "imperial crown style" — the museum's own description: "an Oriental fusion of concrete buildings and roof tiles on top." It was built to commemorate the enthronement of Emperor Showa, took six years, and was made quake- and fire-proof. It became an Important Cultural Property in 2001. ⇒ ⚑ A modern concrete structure wearing a Japanese roof, put up in the late 1930s: the politics of the decade, in a building.

The Hyokeikan was a wedding present. Built for the marriage of the future Emperor Taisho, funded by around 24,000 donors nationwide, designed by Tokuma Katayama of the Imperial Household Ministry on Greek and Roman models. Important Cultural Property since 1978. ⚠ The museum's own two pages give slightly different years for it — completion in 1908 on one, "established 1909" on the other. Both are the museum's; the discrepancy is reported rather than resolved.

And the Gallery of Horyu-ji Treasures was built twice, by a father and a son. The Toyokan of 1968 is by Yoshiro Taniguchi; the Horyu-ji gallery of 1999 is by Yoshio Taniguchi — his son, who later designed MoMA's expansion in New York.

🔴 There is a whole gallery of Horyu-ji here, and you go to Horyu-ji in five days

The museum's own statement: "The Horyuji Treasures consist of over 300 valuable objects, mainly from the 7th–8th century, which were donated to the Imperial Household by Horyuji Temple in 1878."

⇒ 🔴 On 29 October you walk into Horyu-ji itself. Five days earlier you can see three hundred of the things that left it. ⚑ Do this one in the right order and the Nara day gains a whole layer — you will already know what is not in the temple.

The gallery's own predecessor makes the point about fragility: when it first opened in 1964, these objects could be shown only one day a week for conservation reasons. The entire purpose of the 1999 rebuild was to let the same things be shown more often.

Three objects there are worth naming, all on a long rotation running to April 2027 — so they should be up when you are:

🔴 The gilt-bronze kanjo banner, Asuka period, seventh century, a National Treasure — and believed to be the item recorded as "one gilded kanjo ceremony article" in a Horyu-ji property inventory compiled in 747. ⇒ An object you can identify in a list written thirteen hundred years ago.

Forty-eight small gilt-bronze Buddhas, none more than about forty centimetres, believed to have been used for private worship. And a Gigaku dance mask of Karura, the bird-man, seventh century, a National Treasure.

⚠ Everything else rotates. The museum swaps individual objects on schedules from weeks to a year, so no specific item outside that gallery can be promised for any given day.

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

Enormous. Do not attempt all of it. ⚠ You are there on a Saturday, when the museum stays open into the evening. Four things:

1. Go to the Gallery of Horyu-ji Treasures first, not last. ⇒ It is the one part of this museum that is directly about a place you are going to. Find the banner, and remember it appears in a 747 inventory.

2. In the Honkan, stop in the entrance hall before the galleries. The marble staircase around the wellhole is the museum's own named signature spot. ⇒ You are standing inside the 1930s argument about what Japanese modernity should look like.

3. Look at the Honkan from outside, then the Hyokeikan. One is a concrete building with a tiled Japanese roof; the other is a Meiji building on Greek and Roman models. Forty years apart. ⇒ The two buildings disagree with each other, and that disagreement is the museum's real history.

4. The Kuromon, the black gate. The main gate of a daimyo's Edo residence, moved here in 1954 — comparable to the famous Red Gate at Tokyo University. ⚠ It opens only at weekends and holidays and has carried a closure notice; treat it as a bonus, not a plan.

National Museum of Western Art国立西洋美術館
Museum · Ueno · opened 1959 · the only Le Corbusier building in Japan · World Heritage
▶
🔴 The building exists because France made it a condition

This is the strangest founding story of any museum on the trip, and it is told on the museum's own pages.

Kojiro Matsukata (1866–1950), president of Kawasaki Dockyard and son of a prime minister, began buying art in London in the middle of the First World War, funded by wartime shipbuilding profits. His collection reached an estimated ten thousand works. He planned his own museum in Tokyo — the "Sheer Pleasure Fine Arts Pavilion" — designed with the painter Frank Brangwyn.

Then it all came apart. The 1927 financial crisis brought down his company's bank; he resigned and the Japanese portion of the collection was auctioned off. Works stored in a London warehouse burned in a fire in 1939 — the museum's own words: "the details of that loss cannot be confirmed." Around 400 works held in Paris were seized by the French government as enemy property near the end of the Second World War, and became French national property in 1951 under the peace treaty.

🔴 And then France gave most of them back — with a string attached. In June 1953 the French Embassy sent a formal memorandum requiring that Japan build a museum for French art in Tokyo as the condition of return. Le Corbusier was named architect in March 1955. The return was authorised by President de Gaulle in December 1958, signed in January 1959, and the artworks came back to Yokohama by ship. The museum opened that June.

⇒ ⚑ This is a building that was built as a receipt.

National Museum of Western Art official English pages on the Matsukata Collection and the museum's chronology, quoted and paraphrased · checked 2026-08-15

What Le Corbusier built, and why UNESCO named it

The Main Building was completed in March 1959, and the museum describes it as "a symbol of the resumption of diplomatic ties between Japan and France after World War II." Le Corbusier designed it; three of his former students — Junzo Sakakura, Kunio Maekawa and Takamasa Yoshizaka — supervised the work on site. Maekawa also designed the New Wing that opened in 1979.

🔴 In 2016 it was inscribed on the World Heritage list as one of seventeen sites across seven countries in "The Architectural Work of Le Corbusier, an Outstanding Contribution to the Modern Movement." UNESCO's own text singles this building out, calling it "the prototype of the globally transposable Museum of Unlimited Growth."

⇒ The idea is a museum designed as a spiral that could keep being extended outward as the collection grew. ⚠ The museum uses that term for the building, but its own pages do not set out in prose how the spiral is meant to expand — that description comes from UNESCO and from the museum's bibliography rather than from its own explanation.

⚠ One gap worth knowing: the building was designated an Important Cultural Property in 2007 — a fact printed on the museum's Japanese page and absent from its English one.

🔴 The forecourt was restored because UNESCO complained

A small, precise story. When the site was inscribed in 2016, ICOMOS pointed out that Le Corbusier's design intent for the forecourt had been compromised by decades of piecemeal change. The museum used a scheduled roof-waterproofing job as the opportunity, closed the forecourt from October 2020 to April 2022, and restored it "as near as possible to its appearance in 1959."

That meant cutting back planting, returning the main gate to its original position on the west side, and relaying the paving joints according to Le Corbusier's Modulor — his proportional system based on the human body.

⇒ ⚑ The ground you walk across to get in was re-laid to a mathematical scheme, because a heritage body said the yard was wrong.

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

⚠ You are there on the opening day of a major Turner exhibition run with Tate, which will make the special-exhibition wing busy; the permanent collection is a separate ticket and a separate experience. Four things:

1. Stand in the forecourt before you go in and look at the paving joints. Relaid to the Modulor in 2020–22. ⇒ The proportions underfoot are the same system as the building above.

2. The Rodin bronzes in the forecourt. The Gates of Hell, The Thinker, The Burghers of Calais, and Bourdelle's Herakles. ⇒ The line from the original gate to The Gates of Hell was the forecourt's designed axis — that is what the restoration put back.

3. Inside, follow the ramp rather than taking the direct route. ⇒ The spiral is the concept UNESCO named the building for; walking it is the only way to read it.

4. Find the Matsukata works in the permanent collection. ⇒ These are the paintings France seized and gave back, and the reason there is a museum here at all. ⚑ A free display on the museum's own conservation history — including Van Gogh's Roses and Renoir's Woman with a Hat — is running in the Main Building through January 2027.

Yanaka Ginza谷中銀座商店街
Shopping street · Yanaka · about 170 m long · formed around 1945
▶
What it is, and how old it actually is

A shopping street about 170 metres long, five minutes from Nippori station, lined mainly with traditional individually-run shops. The ward's own description of its origin is refreshingly unromantic: it "arose spontaneously around 1945" and developed into a neighbourhood shopping street.

⇒ ⚑ It is not an Edo survival. It is a post-war street that has kept looking like a mid-century one — which is exactly what the ward says: you can see nostalgic Showa-era shopping-street scenery.

Taito City official tourism site (Japanese), quoted and translated · checked 2026-08-15

Why this district looks different from the rest of Tokyo

The ward's own account of the wider Yanaka area: "In the Edo period, temples were gathered here by urban planning, and the area developed as a temple town. Afterward, damage from earthquakes and war was comparatively slight, so the old townscape survives today" — and it has been selected as one of Japan's hundred best-preserved historic landscapes.

⚠ Note what the ward does and does not say. It states the damage was light; it does not explain why. The common explanation — that the temple grounds acted as firebreaks — is not a claim the ward makes, and it is not asserted here.

Taito City official tourism site, area feature page (Japanese), quoted and translated · the absence of a stated cause is noted explicitly · checked 2026-08-15

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

Ten minutes end to end if you do not stop, which you will. Three things:

1. Approach from the Nippori end and stop at the top of the steps. They are called Yuyake Dandan — "sunset steps" — and the ward names them as a spot to watch the sun go down over the old low city. ⇒ Time the day to arrive here late if you can. The street reads best from above.

2. The cats, which are officially a thing. Taito City's own photo captions invite you to "spot the cats" and picture a shop selling baked doughnuts shaped like a cat's tail. ⇒ Not tourist folklore — the ward itself treats the cat theme as a feature of the street.

3. Look at what the shops actually are. Individually run, one trade each, on a street 170 metres long. ⇒ This is the retail form Tokyo mostly stopped having, still operating rather than preserved.

Yanaka Cemetery谷中霊園
Cemetery · Yanaka · opened 1874 · about 10 hectares · the last shogun is buried here
▶
What it is

Opened on 1 September 1874, covering about 102,537 square metres. The managing body's own description is precise about its make-up, and corrects a common simplification: it sits within Yanaka's temple town, and the burial grounds of Kan'ei-ji and Tenno-ji are interwoven within it to form a single cemetery area.

⚠ So it is not simply "the former grounds of Tenno-ji" — it is two temples' graveyards plus a municipal cemetery, tangled together. The north side rises to meet the railway and looks out toward Skytree.

Tokyo Metropolitan Park Association official page for Yanaka Cemetery (Japanese), quoted and translated · checked 2026-08-15

🔴 The last shogun is here, and so is the space where a pagoda stood

Tokugawa Yoshinobu — the fifteenth and final Tokugawa shogun, who surrendered power in 1867 and then lived until 1913 — is buried in this cemetery. He is marked by name on the Park Association's own cemetery map, in the block near the pagoda ruin. ⚑ He does not appear in the map's numbered list of notable graves, which is consistent with his being a family plot rather than a listed monument. ⚠ His grave is often described as an unusual mound rather than a Buddhist stone; that description is not confirmed on the Association's own pages and is left as something to look at rather than a stated fact.

🔴 The pagoda. The Association's page notes the cemetery contains the site of Tenno-ji's five-storey pagoda, which was the model for Koda Rohan's novel The Five-Storied Pagoda. Only the foundation stones remain.

What happened to it: in the early hours of 6 July 1957 it burned. Two bodies were found in the ashes, and the police ruled it an arson-suicide. The pagoda had stood since a rebuilding in 1791, and was one of the four great pagodas of Edo — two of the others had already burned in the air raids of 1945. It was never rebuilt.

⚠ The fire is documented in the contemporary press; a local tradition disputing the official ruling exists but is folklore and is not weighed equally here.

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

A public cemetery you walk through, quiet and open. ⚠ People visit graves here; it is not a park. Four things:

1. The central avenue. The Association singles out its cherry-tree avenue as the finest part of the grounds. ⚠ Bare in late October — but it is the cemetery's own main axis and the easiest way to orient.

2. Yoshinobu's plot. ⇒ The man who ended two and a half centuries of Tokugawa rule is buried in a municipal cemetery in a residential district, a short walk from a shopping street. The scale of the grave against the scale of the office is the thing to notice.

3. The pagoda foundation stones. ⇒ An absence with a novel written about it, and the last of Edo's four great pagodas to go.

4. The other names. The Association's map lists around seventy-six notable graves — among them Shibusawa Eiichi, the industrialist; the painter Yokoyama Taikan; the koto composer Miyagi Michio; and a prime minister. ⇒ A cross-section of the people who built modern Japan, in one ten-hectare block.

Nezu-jinja根津神社
Shrine · Bunkyo · buildings completed 1706 · seven Important Cultural Properties, none missing
▶
🔴 Seven buildings from 1706, and not one of them is gone

This is why the shrine matters in a city that has burned repeatedly. Its own sentence:

"The honden, heiden, haiden, karamon, west gate, latticed fence and romon, completed in Hoei 3 (1706) in the gongen-zukuri form, survive today without a single one missing, and are designated national Important Cultural Properties."

⇒ 🔴 Seven structures, one commission, one date — and the complete set is still standing in central Tokyo.

⚠ But "survive" needs its qualification, and the shrine gives it. The buildings took damage in 1945; restoration was completed in 1959, and repairs to the karamon, fence and romon finished in 1962. ⇒ Original 1706 fabric, repaired after war damage — not untouched.

Nezu-jinja official site, history page (Japanese), the list of seven quoted verbatim and the 1945–1962 restoration sequence, translated · checked 2026-08-15

Why a shogun built it — and it is a family matter

The land here was the residence of Tokugawa Tsunashige, and the birthplace of his son Tsunatoyo, later the sixth shogun Ienobu.

The shrine's account: when the fifth shogun Tsunayoshi designated his elder brother's son Ienobu as his adopted heir, he donated that residence's land to Nezu Gongen-sha — Ienobu's own birth tutelary deity — built the present shrine, moved it here from its old site in 1706, and endowed it with a perpetual estate of 500 koku. The shrine calls the works a great construction of the kind known as a nationwide levy.

⇒ ⚑ A childless shogun adopted his nephew and then rebuilt, at national expense, the shrine of the god who had watched over the boy's birth. The building is an heir announcement.

Nezu-jinja official site, history page (Japanese), quoted and translated · checked 2026-08-15

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

A full Edo shrine complex on a slope, free and open. ⚠ The azalea garden is gated shut outside the April festival — this is an access fact, not just a seasonal one. Five things:

1. The romon, the two-storey gate. The shrine's own claim: the only surviving romon among shrines within Edo. The guardian figure on the right is said to be modelled on Mito Komon. ⇒ A gate type that used to be everywhere in this city and now exists once.

2. Follow the latticed fence around the buildings. Two hundred metres of it, with diamond-lattice openings. The shrine notes a recent survey found its foundations going eight metres underground, and that after three hundred years it shows no distortion. ⇒ Most of this fence is below the ground you are standing on.

3. The torii tunnel up to Otome Inari. The shrine calls the approach a "thousand torii," lined with donated gates. The shrine hut itself is set inside a hollowed-out hole and old records call it "Hole Inari."

4. Look for the swastika crest on the buildings. It was the shrine's crest in the centuries when Buddhism and Shinto were one thing here, and it was left in place when Meiji stripped Buddhist elements out. ⇒ A surviving mark of the older arrangement, on a shrine that was renamed for the new one.

5. The house where Mori Ogai wrote The Dancing Girl. Relocated to the grounds and open only at weekends and holidays — you are there on a Saturday. ⚑ Both Ogai and Natsume Soseki were parishioners here, and a stone in the grounds is said to be the one they sat on.

Monday 26 October — Mount Takao

West of Tokyo · a temple founded by the same emperor and the same monk as the Great Buddha at Nara, on a mountain where two climate zones meet
Takaosan Yakuo-in高尾山薬王院
Shingon temple · Mount Takao · founded 744 · a Shugendo mountain and a tengu mountain
▶
🔴 Founded by the two people behind the Great Buddha

The temple's formal name is Takaosan Yakuo-in Yuki-ji. Its own account of its founding: it was established in 744 by order of Emperor Shomu, with Gyoki as founding priest, as a temple to guard and pray for the eastern provinces. Its original principal image was Yakushi Nyorai, the healing buddha — which is where the name comes from.

The temple's own English page states the connection outright: Gyoki was "a charismatic priest closely associated with the erection of the Great Buddha at Todai-ji Temple in Nara."

⇒ 🔴 On 31 October you stand in front of that Great Buddha. The same emperor ordered both, and the same monk raised both. ⚑ This is the eastern end of a single imperial programme, and Nara is the other end.

Takaosan Yakuo-in official Japanese and English "about" pages, quoted verbatim · checked 2026-08-15

🔴 And it was restarted by a monk from Daigo

The temple declined, and was revived around 1375 by Shungen Daitoku, who is called its second founder. The temple states where he came from: Mount Daigo.

He performed the eight-thousand-stick fire ritual — an extreme form of the esoteric goma — dedicated to Fudo Myo-o, and through it received the vision of Izuna Daigongen, whom he enshrined as the new principal image.

⇒ 🔴 You go to Daigo-ji on 4 November. ⚑ The mountain temple outside Tokyo you climb in week one was restarted by a man from the mountain temple outside Kyoto you climb in week three — the same Shingon mountain tradition, transplanted four hundred kilometres east.

Takaosan Yakuo-in official Japanese "about" pages, on Shungen Daitoku, his origin at Mount Daigo, the eight-thousand-stick rite and the new principal image · checked 2026-08-15

What is worshipped, and it is five gods in one

Izuna Daigongen is not a standard buddha. The temple's own English explanation: it is "a form in which Fudo Myo-o appears to bring people to salvation," and it "combines the elements of five deities: Fudo Myo-o, Karuraten (Garuda, a divine bird), Dakiniten (a demon that feeds on human hearts), Kangiten (a fertility deity with the head of an elephant) and Benzaiten."

⇒ ⚑ A composite made of a wrathful king, a bird, a demon, an elephant-headed god and a goddess of water and music. The cult began at Mount Izuna near Zenko-ji in Nagano and spread nationwide.

🔴 Warlords wore it. The temple records that Takeda Shingen and Uesugi Kenshin venerated Izuna Daigongen as a protector and put it on their helmet crests. The Hojo, who held the Kanto, gave the mountain special protection — partly because it sat in a strategic position.

Takaosan Yakuo-in official English "about" page, quoted verbatim, and the Japanese page for the warlord veneration · checked 2026-08-15

The tengu, and why there are two kinds standing outside

The temple states plainly that Mount Takao has been called a mountain of Shugendo since ancient times, and that practitioners who trained by lying down in the mountains came to be called yamabushi. Its own line on the overlap: the figure of the yamabushi is often identified with the tengu.

🔴 The two statues in front of the Izuna Gongen hall are a hierarchy, and the temple explains it. The smaller one has a crow's beak; the larger has a long nose. In its own English: "The small tengu is considered to be still undergoing religious training, while the large tengu is often likened to an experienced yamabushi who has attained spiritual power."

⇒ ⚑ They are a junior and a senior. Once you know that, every tengu pair you see afterwards reads differently.

Takaosan Yakuo-in official English and Japanese "about" pages, quoted verbatim · checked 2026-08-15

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

The temple sits partway up the main path, which the temple's own trail guide calls mostly paved and easy even for beginners. Four things:

1. At the Joshinmon gate, look for the stone stele beside it. It reads "killing prohibited." The temple cites it as evidence that all taking of life on this mountain was strictly forbidden — for centuries. ⇒ ⚑ The forest you are walking through was protected by religious law long before it was protected by park law.

2. The two tengu at the Izuna hall. Beak and nose. ⇒ Trainee and master.

3. The waterfalls, if the path takes you past one. Biwa-taki and Ja-taki are still working sites of waterfall austerity, and the temple opens them to the public on set days, practitioners in white robes. ⇒ Not a scenic feature. A functioning one.

4. Note what the mountain started. The temple's own trail page states: Mount Takao is the birthplace of Japan's "carry your rubbish home" movement. ⇒ There are no bins, and that is deliberate and historic.

Mount Takao高尾山
Mountain · 599 m · quasi-national park · where two climate zones overlap
▶
🔴 Why the botany here is genuinely exceptional

This is the fact that makes Takao more than a convenient hill. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government's own explanation:

"The forest around Mount Takao sits at the boundary of the cool-temperate and warm-temperate zones, so it has an environment where organisms from both climate zones can grow, and consequently many species of flora and fauna live there."

The numbers that follow from it: 1,300 species of higher plants in 153 families on and around the mountain — and, the striking part, 65 species were first scientifically described from specimens collected here.

⇒ ⚑ Sixty-five plants entered science from this one 599-metre hill. Among them the Takao violet, named for the mountain; the metropolitan government calls Takao "the sacred ground of violets" and counts eighteen violet species inside the park.

The mountain sits in the Meiji no Mori Takao Quasi-National Park, 770 hectares, designated in 1967 as part of the centenary commemoration of the Meiji Restoration.

Tokyo Metropolitan Government Bureau of Environment park pages (the climate boundary and the violets) and the Takao cable-car operator's own page (the species counts), Japanese, translated · checked 2026-08-15

The mountain that stands in for Fuji

The temple's own historical account gives Takao a role in the Edo pilgrimage to Mount Fuji. In the Tenbun era (1532–54), Hojo Ujiyasu had the Fuji deity enshrined on Mount Takao; a hall is said to have stood at a peak called Fujimidai — "Fuji-viewing stand" — where, when the doors were opened, the mountain itself appeared framed like a painting. The trail from the inner sanctuary toward the summit is still called the Fuji road.

🔴 And the framing the temple records is precise: Takao was Fuji's "front stand-in," and Oyama in Sagami its "back stand-in" along the pilgrimage route out of Edo.

⇒ ⚑ People climbed this mountain instead of Fuji, on purpose, and the religion had a word for that. On a clear day the summit gives you Tanzawa and Fuji, and in winter air the Southern Alps.

The cable car, and one claim that does not hold up

The Kiyotaki cable car runs 1,000 metres of track and climbs 271 metres. The operator's own figure for the steepest section is 31 degrees 18 minutes, which it claims is the steepest cable-car gradient in Japan. It opened in 1927, was suspended in 1944 under wartime consolidation, and resumed in 1949.

⚠ And one correction worth making, because you will see the claim everywhere. Takao is often called the most-climbed mountain in the world. What the Tier-1 sources actually say is narrower: the operator gives over 2.5 million visitors a year, and the temple says the annual climber count is first in Japan. ⇒ "Most climbed in Japan" is sourced. "Most climbed in the world" is repeated everywhere and attributed to nobody.

✅ One superlative that does check out: in 2007, the first Japanese edition of the Michelin green guide gave Mount Takao three stars — the only mountains so rated being this one and Fuji.

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

⚠ Trail 1, the main approach, is mostly paved and the temple calls it easy for beginners. The Inari-yama route is described by the temple itself as the hardest. Four things:

1. On the way up, watch the trees change. You are crossing between two climate zones. ⇒ Warm-temperate evergreens low down, cool-temperate species higher — the reason 1,300 plant species live on one small mountain.

2. The suspension bridge on trail 4. The temple's own guide calls it spectacular, and it is the single most distinctive piece of path on the mountain. ⇒ A short detour that changes the walk.

3. At the summit, look south-west and find the Fuji road behind you. ⇒ You have just walked a pilgrimage substitute, and the trail's name still says so.

Thursday 29 October — Ikaruga & Nishinokyō

Nara plain · Horyu-ji in the morning, Yakushi-ji and Toshodai-ji in the afternoon · three temples spanning roughly 150 years of Japanese architecture
Horyu-ji法隆寺
Buddhist temple · Ikaruga, Nara · founded c. 607, rebuilt c. 700
▶
What it is

A walled temple complex on the plain west of Nara, in the town of Ikaruga. Its western precinct — a gate, a pagoda, a golden hall and the roofed corridor that encloses them — is, in the temple's own words, the oldest group of wooden buildings in the world. It is the head temple of the Shotoku school (Shotoku-shu), a position it has held only since 1950.

Official site (temple precinct page) · checked 2026-08-15

Why it was built — a temple as an argument about who governs

Prince Shotoku established his palace at Ikaruga in 601, and the temple was finished around 607 under Empress Suiko, having been begun for his father, Emperor Yomei.

The timing is the whole point. Shotoku was regent in the decades when the Japanese court was still deciding whether Buddhism was a foreign cult to be suppressed or the working equipment of a centralised state — a question the Soga and Mononobe clans had fought over with weapons a generation earlier. Putting a monumental Buddhist complex directly beside the regent's own residence was not private devotion. It was a public statement that the new religion and the new form of government had arrived together, and that the court was on the Buddhist side of a fight that had recently been lethal.

Official site (precinct page) · Prince Shotoku and Empress Suiko, English Wikipedia

What is worshipped here — and the school it walked away from

For most of its history Horyu-ji belonged to the Hosso school — the Japanese form of Yogacara Buddhism, whose central claim is that what we take for an external world is a construction of consciousness. It is one of the most demanding philosophical positions in East Asian Buddhism, and it was the intellectual tradition of this temple for over a thousand years.

In 1950 Horyu-ji left it and declared itself head of a new school named after Prince Shotoku. That is an unusual thing to do. Japanese Buddhist schools normally take a buddha, a bodhisattva or a body of scripture as their centre; this one takes a dead statesman. It was not invented from nothing — devotion to Shotoku as a semi-divine figure (Taishi shinko, "faith in the Prince") had been growing since the Heian period, crediting him with visions, prophecy and earlier lives in China. What 1950 did was make it official. ⇒ The move formalised something Japanese religion had been doing quietly for a millennium: turning a political founder into a religious one.

Worth carrying into the afternoon: Yakushi-ji, which you see after lunch, is one of the two head temples of the Hosso school Horyu-ji walked out of. The two temples are twenty minutes apart and on opposite sides of that 1950 decision.

Official site (school affiliation) · Hosso / East Asian Yogacara, English Wikipedia · Japanese-language reference works on the Shotoku school (no English edition found)

Are these really the original buildings? — a forty-year argument, ended by a spade

From the Meiji period onward Japanese historians split bitterly over one question. The Nihon Shoki, the 8th-century imperial chronicle, records that the temple at Ikaruga burned to the ground in 670. But the buildings standing here look older than anything that could have been raised after that date.

One camp trusted the document and argued for a rebuild; the other trusted the buildings and argued the chronicle was wrong. The historian Kita Sadakichi put the stakes for his whole discipline in a single sentence: if you are permitted to reject the documents, there is no such thing as history.

It ran for roughly four decades and ended in 1939, when the archaeologist Ishida Mosaku excavated inside the precinct and found the foundations of an earlier temple on a different alignment — pagoda and golden hall set one behind the other in the older continental arrangement, rather than side by side as they are above ground. The burned temple had been lying underneath the whole time. ⇒ The buildings you walk through are a rebuild of the late 7th or early 8th century — and they are still the oldest surviving wooden buildings on earth. Both camps were half right, and only digging could tell which half.

Architecture and how the buildings were used

The layout breaks the rule it inherited. Chinese monasteries of the period put the pagoda and the main hall on a single axis, one behind the other. Horyu-ji sets them side by side. The two buildings do different jobs — a pagoda is a reliquary holding the Buddha's physical remains, a golden hall holds his image — and placing them as equals rather than in sequence is a decision about their relative standing. It is an early, visible case of continental architecture being rearranged on Japanese ground rather than copied.

The pagoda is a container, not a room. About 32.5 m tall, built around a central pillar (shinbashira) cut from a cypress felled in 594. The relics it exists to house are said to be buried roughly three metres beneath its base. You do not go inside it.

The golden hall is where the temple actually worked. A two-storey hip-and-gable roof over a lower skirt roof (mokoshi), deep eaves carried on cloud-shaped bracket arms (kumo-hijiki) — a support form the temple states is unique to the Asuka period, and therefore one of the clearest ways to date a building by eye. Inside stand the Shakyamuni triad, a Yakushi (the healing buddha), an Amida triad, the Four Heavenly Kings and a Kisshoten. This was the room where the temple's rites were performed and where its monks studied the Hosso doctrine — not a museum arrangement but a working hall that stopped being renovated.

The fire that wrote a national law

On the morning of 26 January 1949, while the golden hall was dismantled for repair and painters were at work copying the murals inside it, fire broke out and destroyed most of them — a cycle of 7th-century Buddhist wall paintings regarded as the finest in East Asia.

Until then, protecting cultural property in Japan was largely left to whoever happened to own it. The year after the fire the government passed the Cultural Properties Protection Law (30 May 1950), putting the state in charge of designating and funding preservation. That is the law under which every "National Treasure" and "Important Cultural Property" label elsewhere in this almanac is issued. In 1955 the date was made permanent as the national Cultural Properties Fire Prevention Day, and the Agency for Cultural Affairs still gives the reason plainly: it is the day the golden hall of Horyu-ji burned, and January and February are when fires are most likely.

⇒ The system Japan uses to protect its heritage was written in response to what burned at this one building. The salvaged murals have been kept in a purpose-built storehouse on the grounds since 1952.

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

Two walled precincts — western (the old one) and eastern, built round the octagonal Hall of Dreams — with the Gallery of Temple Treasures between them. It is large and flat; the walking is easy and the ground is gravel throughout. Six things worth stopping for, roughly in the order you meet them:

1. The middle gate — look at the middle of the doorway. There is a pillar standing in the centre of the entrance. You cannot walk straight through the front gate of this temple; you go round one side or the other. No other major Japanese temple gate does this, and the temple's own explanation is structural — the gate is so wide it needed an extra support. It is the single most argued-over detail in the complex, and it is the first thing you see.

2. The brackets under every set of eaves — the ones shaped like clouds. Look up where the roof overhangs, on the gate, the golden hall and the pagoda alike. The stacked arms carrying that overhang curl into cloud forms (kumo-hijiki), and the temple states this shape is unique to Asuka-period architecture. ⇒ This is the useful one, because you are going to Toshodai-ji this afternoon, where the same job is done by a squared-off three-tiered bracket system that its temple names as the marker of its century. Look up at both and you can see roughly 150 years of structural engineering change with your own eyes, without knowing anything else.

3. The corridor pillars — look for the swelling. The roofed corridor enclosing the western precinct runs on columns that bulge slightly at mid-height, a shaping called entasis. It is the same optical correction used on Greek columns. Easiest to see by sighting along the row rather than staring at one column — and you will meet the idea again this afternoon, where Toshodai-ji's own pages compare its colonnade to the Parthenon.

4. The base of the pagoda — go round all four sides. The tower itself you cannot enter, but at ground level, set into the four faces, are cavern-like tableaux made in 711 holding 97 small clay figures. Each face is a different scene: the north shows the Buddha's death with his disciples mourning and is the one people stop at; the west shows his relics being divided up; the east, a layman debating with the bodhisattva of wisdom; the south, the future buddha preaching. Most visitors see one side and move on.

5. Inside the golden hall — two sculptural styles in one room. The Shakyamuni triad is early 7th century: frontal, symmetrical, flat-draped, faces held in a fixed archaic smile — a continental idiom arriving more or less intact. The Amida triad in the same hall is later and visibly softer and rounder. ⇒ Standing between them you are looking at the moment Japanese sculpture stopped copying and started adapting, in one room, without a museum label telling you so.

6. The Hall of Dreams — and the statue that is normally sealed. The octagonal hall in the eastern precinct was commissioned in 739 by the scholar-priest Gyoshin, on the site of Prince Shotoku's own palace, where he died in 622. Inside is the Kuse Kannon: 179 cm, carved from a single block of camphor wood and gilded head to toe. It is a hidden image — the temple shows it for about a month in spring and about a month in autumn, and otherwise it is closed away. 🔴 The autumn opening runs 22 October to 22 November, and you are there on 29 October. It will be open. This is the one thing at Horyu-ji you could not see on most days of the year.

Why it was built — a vow made over a sickbed

The temple states its own founding plainly: it was established in 680 by Emperor Tenmu to pray for the recovery of his consort from illness — the consort who would herself later reign as Empress Jito.

The dedication is not incidental. Yakushi is the Medicine Buddha, the buddha of healing, and a temple raised to him for a specific sick person is a vow in architecture — the scale of the building standing in for the scale of the request. The emperor died before it was finished; his widow, the patient, completed it. ⇒ The temple outlived both the illness and the marriage that prompted it, which is roughly the pattern of Japanese state Buddhism in this century: private imperial vows hardening into permanent public institutions.

What is taught here — the school Horyu-ji left

Yakushi-ji is a head temple of the Hosso school — Japanese Yogacara, the "consciousness-only" tradition, which holds that the world we take to be outside us is produced by mind. It is a philosophical position rather than a devotional one, and it made Hosso the most academic of the old Nara schools: a temple like this was as much a doctrinal college as a place of worship.

This is the direct counterpart to the morning. Horyu-ji belonged to this same school for over a thousand years and left it in 1950 to found a school centred on Prince Shotoku. Yakushi-ji stayed. ⇒ Two temples twenty minutes apart, one of which walked out of the tradition the other still heads — and the visitor sees both between breakfast and dusk.

Hosso / East Asian Yogacara, English Wikipedia · school affiliation from both temples' own pages

One original building, and a temple rebuilt out of handwriting

Fire and war took nearly everything. The East Pagoda is the only wooden structure on the site to have survived since the founding — the temple says so in exactly those terms, and it has stood for over 1,300 years.

What is unusual is how the rest came back. From 1968 the temple funded its reconstruction through o-shakyo — donations made by copying out sutras by hand. Members of the public paid to trace scripture, and the accumulated sums rebuilt the main hall, the West Pagoda and the Great Lecture Hall across the following decades. ⇒ A 7th-century layout was restored in the 20th century by an act of medieval devotional practice, performed at scale by ordinary people. It is one of the few major Japanese temples whose modern buildings have a social history rather than just a budget.

Official site, English edition (O-Shakyo; About) · reconstruction sequence from the trip's own verified record (2026-08-08)

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

Compact and flat, one minute from the station, and it sits in the same afternoon as Toshodai-ji ten minutes up the lane. Four things worth stopping for:

1. Count the roofs on the East Pagoda — then count the floors. It is 33.6 m tall and it reads as a six-storey tower. It has three storeys. Each real roof is paired with a smaller skirt roof (mokoshi) beneath it, so the eye is given six edges and the building has half that many levels. The temple calls the effect a "grand rhythmical balance", and the art historian Ernest Fenollosa is credited — by the temple itself — with calling it "frozen music". ⇒ Stand back far enough to see the whole silhouette before you go close. This is the one building here that is actually 1,300 years old, and the trick it plays is the reason it is famous.

2. Look at the very top of that pagoda. The finial carries a bronze openwork ornament called a suien, a "water flame", cut with celestial figures dancing and playing flutes. It is not decoration: it is a charm against fire, water set at the summit of a wooden tower. ⇒ Given that fire is what removed every other original building on this site, the charm on the one survivor is worth thirty seconds of looking up.

3. Compare the two pagodas deliberately. East is 8th century; West is a 1981 reconstruction. They face each other, same design, ~1,250 years apart — and the new one is painted in the bright vermilion and green the old one has lost. ⇒ This is the clearest before-and-after in Nara: the west tower shows you what the east tower looked like when it was new, and the east shows you what thirteen centuries of weather does. Very few sites let you see both states of the same building at once.

4. The Yakushi Triad in the main hall. Bronze, from the founding period, and now almost black — the temple calls them "the world's most beautiful Buddhist statues", which is a claim, but the thing to look at is the surface: this is what unrestored ancient bronze becomes. Note the pedestal of the central figure, carved with foreign motifs — the mixed vocabulary of the Silk Road arriving at the eastern end of it.

Toshodai-ji唐招提寺
Buddhist temple · Nishinokyo, Nara · founded 759 by the Chinese monk Ganjin
▶
What it is

The temple founded by Ganjin (Chinese: Jianzhen), the monk invited from China to fix something the Japanese Buddhist establishment could not fix itself. Its Golden Hall is described by the temple as the greatest surviving structure of the Tempyo era — the 8th century — and it is the quietest of the three temples you see today.

Official site, English edition · checked 2026-08-15

Why it exists — a bureaucratic crisis solved by a blind man who crossed the sea

By the mid-8th century Japan had temples, sutras and monks in quantity, and a problem underneath all of it: nobody in the country was properly qualified to ordain anyone. Buddhist ordination requires a quorum of already-ordained masters conferring the precepts in an unbroken line back to India. Japan did not have that line, which meant its entire clergy was, in strict terms, self-appointed — and since ordained monks were exempt from tax and labour, this was as much a fiscal problem for the state as a doctrinal one for the church.

Emperor Shomu invited Ganjin to come and supply the missing link. The temple's own account of the journey is stark: twelve years, five failed attempts, and the loss of his eyesight, before he finally crossed the ocean and reached Nara in 754. He was in his sixties and blind when he arrived.

He then did exactly what he had been brought for: an ordination platform was built in front of the Great Buddha Hall at Todai-ji, and he administered the precepts there — not only to senior Japanese priests but to the retired Emperor Shomu and Empress Koken in person. ⇒ The legitimacy of the Japanese Buddhist clergy dates from a ceremony conducted by a blind foreigner who had spent twelve years failing to get there. Toshodai-ji was given to him in 759 as his own base.

Official site, English edition (About Toshodaiji) · checked 2026-08-15

What is taught here — rules, not metaphysics

Ganjin's school is Ritsu — vinaya, the monastic discipline: the body of rules governing how a monk lives, eats, dresses and is ordained. The temple states it was the first temple in Japan devoted to one of the Chinese Buddhist denominations.

This is worth holding next to the morning. Horyu-ji and Yakushi-ji represent Hosso — a school built on a philosophical claim about the nature of mind. Ritsu is built on procedure. Its concern is not what is ultimately real but whether the ceremony was performed correctly by people entitled to perform it. ⇒ In one day you see both of the things a religion needs in order to become an institution: a doctrine, and a rulebook.

Official site, English edition (About; Ritsu School Academy)

Architecture — the building most often called the most beautiful in Japan

The Golden Hall is late 8th century, National Treasure, with a hipped tile roof. Its front is seven bays wide, and the bays are not equal: the central one is about 4.7 m and they narrow steadily to about 3.3 m at the ends. The eaves are carried on a three-tiered bracket system (mitesaki), which the temple names as the marker of its construction period.

The front bay is open — a colonnade with no wall, so the building presents a row of freestanding columns rather than a facade. The temple's own pages compare those columns to the Parthenon. ⇒ That comparison is the temple's, not this almanac's, and it is doing real work: the columns swell at mid-height in the same way Horyu-ji's corridor pillars do, and the open colonnade makes the effect impossible to miss.

Official site: Golden Hall page (bay widths, mitesaki brackets, open front bay) · English edition (the Parthenon comparison) · checked 2026-08-15

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

Ten minutes on foot north of Yakushi-ji, on the same lane. Wooded, level, and much less visited than the other two. Four things:

1. Stand square in front of the Golden Hall before going in. This is the view the building was designed around, and the thing to notice is the narrowing bays — the columns are not evenly spaced. The centre is wide and the spacing tightens towards the edges, which pulls the eye inward and makes the hall read as larger and more settled than its actual width. It is a deliberate optical effect, and once you see it you cannot stop seeing it.

2. Look up at the brackets, then remember this morning. Three squared tiers stepping outward to carry the eaves. At Horyu-ji the same job was done by cloud-shaped arms, which that temple states are unique to the Asuka period. Both temples publish the fact that their own bracket form dates them. ⇒ Roughly 150 years separate the two systems and you have seen both in one afternoon — this is the single easiest way to learn to date a Japanese building by eye.

3. Inside the hall — count the arms. Three great figures stand together: the Rushana Buddha in the centre, over 3 m tall with a halo 5.15 m high; the Yakushi Nyorai at right, 3.36 m; and at left the Thousand-armed Kannon, 5.36 m — which was built with a thousand arms and now has 953: 42 large and 911 small. ⇒ The number is exact and the temple publishes it. Nearly fifty arms have been lost over twelve centuries, and knowing that changes what you are looking at from a symbol into an object with a history of damage.

4. The lecture hall behind it was not built as a temple. It was an administrative building of the imperial palace, given to Ganjin by the court and physically moved here. ⇒ It is the only surviving building from the Nara palace complex, and it is standing in a temple because the government gave a monk an office block. What you are looking at is secular 8th-century state architecture.

⚑ What you will not see: Ganjin's dry-lacquer portrait statue — made by his own disciples, a National Treasure, and one of the oldest portrait sculptures in Japan — is kept in the Miei-do and shown only a few days a year, around 6 June. His tomb is in the eastern woods and can be walked to.

Isonokami Jingu石上神宮
Shinto shrine · Tenri, Nara · the northern end of the walk
▶
What it is

One of the two shrines the 8th-century chronicles call jingu — the other is Ise. It sits in cedar woods above Tenri, its worship hall is a National Treasure, and long-tailed roosters wander loose on the approach. Admission is free and there is no interior to visit.

Tenri city tourism office · the trip's own verified record (2026-07-29)

Why the state cared about it — a shrine that was an armoury

This was the shrine of the Mononobe, the clan whose hereditary business was arms and enforcement, and it functioned as the weapons store of the Yamato court. That is an unusual job for a sanctuary and it explains what is kept here: iron shields, armour, swords.

The Mononobe are also the clan that lost. They led the faction that fought to keep Buddhism out of Japan and were destroyed by the pro-Buddhist Soga in 587 — the same conflict whose winning side built Horyu-ji, which you saw yesterday. ⇒ In two days you visit the temple that the victory built and the shrine of the family that was beaten.

Tenri city tourism office · Mononobe–Soga conflict, English Wikipedia

The god that was dug up

Isonokami originally had no main hall. Behind the worship hall lay a forbidden precinct — ground nobody was permitted to enter — and the tradition held that the shrine's sacred sword was buried in it.

In August 1874 the chief priest obtained government permission and dug it up. He found beads, ring-pommel swords, stone objects, gilt-bronze ornaments — and a sword, taken to be the Futsu-no-Mitama of the tradition. A second sword was found in a further dig in 1878. A main hall was then built to house what had been excavated, and finished in 1913.

⇒ Read that sequence again. The shrine spent most of its existence worshipping a patch of forbidden ground, then a Meiji priest excavated the ground, found the god, and built the god a house. It is one of the very few places where you can date the moment an ancient form of worship was replaced by a modern one — and the replacement was performed by the shrine's own clergy, with a spade and a government permit. The excavated objects are Important Cultural Properties and are held at the Nara National Museum, which you visit tomorrow.

Agency for Cultural Affairs heritage database · Japanese-language shrine histories · designation 28 December 1897

The seven-branched sword

The shrine's most famous object is the Shichishito — a 4th-century iron blade with six branches projecting from a central shaft, a shape with no practical use whatever. For a thousand years it was assumed to be an odd halberd.

Then, in the 1870s, the chief priest Kan Masatomo (1824–1897) — the same man who dug up the forbidden precinct — brushed the rust off and found an inscription inlaid in gold, running across both faces. It is corroded, partly unreadable, and scholars have argued over it for more than a century. What it seems to record is that the sword was a gift from the king of Baekje, a kingdom on the Korean peninsula.

⇒ This is why the object matters far beyond the shrine: the wording decides what kind of relationship Baekje and early Japan had — whether tribute, alliance or something else — and the wording is exactly what cannot be read with certainty. An 8th-century Japanese chronicle records a "seven-branched sword" presented in the fifty-second year of Empress Jingu's reign; if that is this sword, it dates to the later 4th century. ⛔ You will not see it. It is a National Treasure, extremely fragile — a few millimetres thick at its thinnest — and is kept in climate-controlled storage.

Japan Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism — official multilingual commentary database, "Shichishito Sword" (English) · checked 2026-08-15

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

Free, open air, no ticket. You reach it early in the day, at the top of the walk. Three things:

1. The roosters. Long-tailed birds roam the cedar approach and are not decoration — chickens are messengers of the gods in Shinto, and these are kept, not tolerated. They are the first thing you meet and the thing you will remember.

2. The worship hall, and the emptiness behind it. The haiden is a National Treasure. Stand in front of it and note that you are facing the direction of the forbidden precinct — the ground that was, for most of this shrine's life, the entire object of worship. The building behind it exists only because that ground was opened in 1874.

3. The subordinate Izumo-Takeo shrine's worship hall is also a National Treasure — two National Treasure halls on one free, unticketed site, which is rare enough to be worth the detour to see the second.

Chogaku-ji長岳寺
Buddhist temple · Tenri, Nara · founded 824 · the midpoint of the walk
▶
What it is

The one Buddhist stop on a road otherwise made of shrines, and the natural halfway halt. Founded in 824 by Kukai under a decree of Emperor Junna.

Temple's own site · Japanese-language reference works · checked 2026-08-15

Why a temple is standing in a shrine's grounds

Chogaku-ji was built as the jingu-ji — the shrine-temple — of Oyamato Shrine, further north on this same road. ⚠ Not Omiwa at the southern end; a different shrine, easily confused.

The jingu-ji is one of the strangest and most revealing institutions in Japanese religion: a Buddhist temple built inside or beside a Shinto shrine in order to preach to the shrine's god. The logic was that the kami were themselves unenlightened beings trapped in the cycle of rebirth, and therefore needed the sutras read to them. For roughly a thousand years this was normal, and Japan's religious landscape was a single hybrid.

⇒ It ended by government order. The Meiji separation of Buddhism and Shinto (from 1868) pulled the two apart by force, and most jingu-ji were dissolved. That this one survives at all, on a road whose other stops are shrines, is the visible remnant of the thousand-year arrangement — and it is why the walk feels doctrinally incoherent if you assume Shinto and Buddhism were ever really separate.

Temple's own site (Japanese): "founded by Kobo Daishi as the jingu-ji of Oyamato Shrine" · shinbutsu bunri, English Wikipedia

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

Paid, small, about twenty minutes unless the screen reading is running. Three things, and the first is a genuine landmark in the history of sculpture:

1. The Amida triad — look at the eyes. Dated by inscriptions inside the statues to 1151, and it is the oldest securely dated example in Japan of gyokugan: the technique of setting crystal into the eyes from behind, so that they catch light and appear wet. ⇒ Before this, Buddhist figures had carved or painted eyes and looked like carvings. After it, they look back at you. You are standing in front of the moment Japanese sculpture became uncanny, and the room is small enough to see it properly.

2. The bell-tower gate — an Important Cultural Property, a gate and a belfry in one structure, which the temple claims is the oldest of its kind in the country. You walk under the bell to get in.

3. 🔴 The hell screen — and you are inside the window. The temple owns a large hell painting by Kano Sanraku, roughly 11 m by 4 m. For most of the year the main hall shows a replica. From 23 October to the end of November the original is hung in its place — and you are here on 30 October. ⚑ The head priest reads the painting aloud, scene by scene, at 1 pm on Saturdays and Sundays; the 30th is a Friday, so there is no scheduled reading — on weekdays it happens only when a group has booked one, and individuals may sit in.

What it is

A clearing on the hillside with a torii standing in the open and nothing behind it. No main hall, no worship hall, no roof. What the torii frames is Mount Miwa, and the mountain is what is being worshipped.

Omiwa Shrine · the trip's own verified record (2026-07-29)

Why it is called the original Ise

Amaterasu, the sun goddess and ancestress of the imperial line, was originally worshipped inside the palace itself. According to the chronicles, Emperor Sujin came to find this unbearable — the account is that he feared the power of a god kept under his own roof — and had her removed.

He entrusted the sacred regalia to his daughter Toyosukiiri-hime, who enshrined the goddess out here, in the open, at a place the chronicles call Yamato Kasanui-no-mura. This site is held to be that place. The charge later passed to Yamato-hime, who carried the goddess on a long search for a permanent home and finally settled her at the Isuzu river in Ise.

⇒ This is where the Grand Shrine of Ise began, and it is a field with a gate in it. The shrine the Association of Shinto Shrines was founded revering as its head — it says so in its own founding sentence — started as a temporary arrangement for a goddess an emperor was afraid to live with. ⚑ The rite for Toyosukiiri-hime is held in this precinct on 5 November at 11:00 — six days after you pass through.

What it is

The shrine describes itself as the oldest in the country, and its claim rests on something you can verify by looking: there is no main hall, and there never has been. In its own words, "there is no main shrine even today, and the ancient form of worshipping has been handed down as it was."

What is being worshipped — the mountain, not a building

Behind the worship hall stands Mount Miwa, and the mountain is the shrine. The English page puts it directly: "the mountain itself makes the main shrine where the divine spirit is enshrined" — a shintai-zan, a mountain-as-divine-body, about 16 km around.

⇒ This is the reason the place matters. Almost every Shinto shrine you will ever enter is a building containing a god. This is a god containing a building. The architecture stops at the worship hall because there is nothing to put the god in — the god is already there, and is roughly four million square metres of forest.

The god — and why sake brewers come here

The deity is Omononushi. The shrine's own account has him working with Sukunahikona to cultivate the land and to establish "every industry such as agriculture, industry and commerce", and lists his particular concerns as curing disease, brewing, medicine-making and marriage.

⇒ That combination is not random. Brewing and medicine are the same skill in an early agricultural society — controlled fermentation, applied to grain or to a body — and a god who governs both is a god of the technologies that made settled life possible. ⚑ This is why Omiwa is the shrine of the sake trade: brewers across Japan hang a ball of cedar sprigs outside the door when a new batch is ready, and the cedar comes from this mountain.

Saturday 31 October — Todai-ji, the Shoso-in Exhibition and Kasuga

Nara Park · the state religion of the 8th century, its treasury, and the private shrine of the family that ran it
Todai-ji東大寺
Buddhist temple · Nara · founded 8th century · head temple of the Kegon school
▶
What it is

The temple built to hold a bronze Buddha roughly 15 m high, in a hall that is among the largest wooden buildings in the world. Head temple of the Kegon school — the Japanese transmission of Huayan Buddhism, whose governing idea is that every thing that exists contains and reflects every other.

Official site, English edition · checked 2026-08-15

Why it was built — a statue commissioned against a catastrophe

This is the part usually left out. Emperor Shomu did not order the Great Buddha out of piety in a settled reign. The temple's own English history names what he was facing: "struggles for political authority, small pox epidemics, and rebellions", and says he came to believe "religion was the only means to heal such troubled times and to seek peace for the nation."

The smallpox epidemic of the 730s is estimated to have killed a substantial share of the population and it destroyed the governing class — including all four Fujiwara brothers who dominated the court. In 741 Shomu ordered a national network of provincial monasteries, the kokubun-ji, one per province. In 743 he issued the edict for the Buddha itself.

⇒ What you are standing in front of is a public-health and political emergency, converted into bronze. The bronze took three years to cast and was finished in 749; the hall in 751; the consecration was held in 752. Tōdai-ji sat at the head of the kokubun-ji system as "a center for rituals for the peace of the nation and the prosperity of the people" — a national temple in the literal administrative sense.

Official site, English edition — History, 8th century · checked 2026-08-15

Why this Buddha, and not another

The figure is Vairocana — in Japanese Rushana — and the choice is doctrinal, not aesthetic. In the Avatamsaka (Flower Garland) Sutra that the Kegon school is built on, Vairocana is the cosmic buddha of whom every other buddha and every phenomenon is an expression. Shomu's edict, as the temple quotes it, invokes "perfectly harmonious worlds" in which "one encounters no obstacles", and extends the benefit to "all in heaven and on earth, even animals and plants."

⇒ The theology and the politics are the same statement. A doctrine in which everything interpenetrates and nothing obstructs is a very convenient doctrine for a ruler trying to hold a fractured state together — and a colossal Vairocana at the centre of a network of provincial temples is that doctrine built at scale, in metal, where everyone can see it.

Official site, English edition — History, 8th century (the edict's language) · checked 2026-08-15

Who actually built it

Two names. Roben (689–773) was the first chief abbot. Gyoki (668–749) was the one who made it possible: an itinerant monk with an enormous popular following, who had spent his career building roads, bridges and irrigation works for ordinary people — and who had earlier been suppressed by the same state for preaching to commoners without authorisation.

The court then needed him. The temple's phrasing is that Gyoki "regimented the efforts of the people through the solicitation of donations." ⇒ The state could order a colossal statue but could not pay for it, so it rehabilitated a monk it had persecuted and used his popularity to raise the money and the labour. Gyoki died in 749, three years before the consecration.

Official site, English edition — History, 8th century · checked 2026-08-15

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

You enter through the park, and the day is long. Four things:

1. The Great South Gate, before you reach the hall. Two guardian figures roughly 8.4 m tall stand inside it, carved by Unkei and Kaikei's workshop in 1203. They were assembled in about 69 days. ⇒ Look at the muscle and the twist: this is Kamakura-period sculpture reacting against the calm frontality you saw at Horyu-ji two days ago, and the contrast is the clearest in Japan.

2. The hall is smaller than it was, and you can see the joint. The current Great Buddha Hall is a 1709 rebuild after the earlier halls burned in 1180 and 1567. It was rebuilt at about six-tenths of the original width — eleven bays reduced to seven — because the money was not there, but at the original height and depth. ⇒ So the building is disproportionate on purpose: too tall and deep for its frontage. Once you know, the squatness of the original is impossible to un-see, and the shortfall is a budget, preserved in timber for three centuries.

3. The octagonal lantern in front of the hall. It is 8th century — original, from the founding, unlike almost everything around it — and its bronze panels show celestial musicians playing flutes. It stands in the open where everyone walks past it to photograph the hall behind.

4. The Buddha's own body is a patchwork of disasters. The seated figure is 14.98 m and was cast in 749, but only parts are original: the head is an Edo-period recasting and the hands are Momoyama. ⇒ You are looking at a Nara-period body with a 17th-century head, and the seams are the two burnings.

What it is, and why it is the important one

The oldest building at Todai-ji, dated to around 733 — which is to say it predates the temple. It was a main hall of Konshusen-ji, the monastery that stood on this hillside before Todai-ji existed and was absorbed into it.

⇒ Everything downhill from here — the Great Buddha, the hall, the national temple system — was added later, around this. ⚑ And the Kegon sutra, the text on which the whole enterprise below is founded, is said to have been expounded in Japan for the first time inside this building. The doctrine arrived in this room; the state built the statue afterwards.

What is inside — the densest room of National Treasures in Japan

Ten Nara-period statues, every one of them a National Treasure, standing crowded together in a small dark hall. At the centre is the Fukukensaku Kannon — the Kannon "whose rope never fails to catch", a net cast to save beings — which gave the hall its older name.

Around it are the two Kongo Rikishi guardians and the Four Heavenly Kings. One figure, the Shukongojin, is a hidden image and is unveiled on 16 December only — you will not see it. ⇒ This is the difference between a hall and a museum, and it is worth noticing which you prefer: the statues here stand in the building they were made for, at the spacing they were made for, in its light. Nigatsu-do二月堂
Hall of Todai-ji · Nara · present building 1669 · home of a rite begun in 752
▶
What it is

A hall on the hillside above the Great Buddha, standing on a pillared platform that projects out over the slope and looks west across the whole Nara basin. There is no gate and no fee; the temple states it can be visited at any hour, day or night.

Official site, English edition · the trip's own verified record (2026-08-08)

The building exists to house a ceremony, not the other way round

Its name means Second Month Hall, after the Shuni-e rite held here in the second month of the old calendar — now 1–14 March. The temple dates the rite's beginning to 752, the same year the Great Buddha was consecrated, and names its founder as Jitchu, chief disciple of Todai-ji's first abbot.

The rite is a repentance offered to the Eleven-headed Kannon — and crucially, it is not performed on the participants' own behalf. In the temple's words it addresses the state's spiritual ills so as to "guarantee the welfare of the people." Eleven monks, the Rengyoshu, carry it out, with about thirty people involved in total.

⇒ That is the thing worth carrying away: a fortnight of atonement performed annually, by eleven men, on behalf of everyone in the country, whether or not anyone is watching. ⚑ It is widely said the rite has never once been missed since 752; the temple's own pages do not make that claim, so it is recorded here as what it is — a statement repeated everywhere rather than one sourced from the temple.

What happens in it

Two elements are famous. The dattan, in which a great pine torch is swung about inside the inner sanctum — fire, indoors, in a wooden National Treasure, deliberately. ⚑ The hall you are standing on burned in 1667 during the ceremony and was rebuilt two years later. ⇒ The building's own history is the risk the rite runs, and the rite continued anyway.

What this actually is

Behind Todai-ji stands the Shoso-in, a raised log storehouse that was the temple's treasury and is now administered by the Imperial Household Agency. It holds roughly 9,000 objects from the 8th century.

They are there because of a death. When Emperor Shomu — the man who ordered the Great Buddha — died, his widow Empress Komyo dedicated his personal possessions to the Buddha he had built, on five separate occasions beginning 21 June 756.

⇒ This is not an archaeological collection. Nothing here was excavated, lost or rediscovered. These are one man's belongings, given away by his wife, and kept in the same building ever since — an 8th-century household with an unbroken chain of custody of nearly thirteen centuries. There is nothing else like it anywhere in the world.

Shoso-in Exhibition official site (about) · Todai-ji official site, English edition (the repository) · checked 2026-08-15

Why it is open now, and only now

The repository is not a museum and does not have opening hours. Its imperial seal is ceremonially broken once a year, each autumn, so the treasures can be inventoried — and the exhibition exists to show a rotating handful of them during that window.

⇒ You are not visiting a museum that happens to have a good show on. You are here in the few weeks of the year the box is legally open, and the objects you see will go back inside and be resealed. A different selection appears each year, so a given object may not surface again for decades.

The Silk Road claim, corrected

The collection is famous as a Silk Road treasury, and objects from Tang China, Unified Silla, Sassanid Persia and Southeast Asia are genuinely in it. But the exhibition's own figure is worth knowing before you go in: about 95 percent of the objects were made in 8th-century Japan.

⇒ ⚑ That is the more interesting fact, not the less. The imports matter because of what surrounds them — a working domestic material culture of furniture, weapons, medicine, musical instruments, masks, textiles and paperwork — and the foreign pieces are the exceptions that show how far the trade reached. A room of only Persian glass would tell you nothing about Nara.

Kurama-dera鞍馬寺
Mountain temple · north of Kyoto · founded 770 · head temple of its own school since 1949
▶
What it is

A temple climbing the side of a mountain above Kurama village, entered through a gate at the bottom and worked upward on foot. It is the head temple of Kurama-kokyo — a school it founded itself, in living memory.

Official site (Japanese): history and teaching pages · checked 2026-08-15

Founded by a student of the man whose temple you saw on Thursday

The temple's own history opens with a name you have already met. Gantei, who founded this place in 770, is described as "a high disciple of the venerable Ganjin of Toshodai-ji in Nara" — the blind Chinese monk who spent twelve years and five failed crossings getting to Japan, whose temple you walked through on 29 October.

The founding account is a dream and an animal: Gantei climbed the mountain "guided by a dream revelation and a white horse" on the fourth night of the first month. In 796 the story repeats almost exactly — Fujiwara no Isendo, the official in charge of building To-ji, came up the same mountain with the same supernatural guidance, intending to enshrine Kannon. A second dream told him "Bishamonten and Kannon are at root one and the same", so both were installed.

⇒ ⚑ That last line is not a folk detail; it is the temple's founding doctrinal move, and everything below follows from it. Two deities that other traditions keep separate were declared aspects of one thing here, in the 8th century.

Official site (Japanese), history page — quoted and translated · checked 2026-08-15

What is actually worshipped here, and it is not a buddha

The object of devotion is Sonten, which the temple defines in its own words as "the universal life and cosmic energy that produces all existence in this world, beginning with humanity." Sonten has three aspects, and the temple sets them out as a table:

  • Love — the spirit of the moon — Senju Kannon
  • Light — the spirit of the sun — Bishamonten
  • Power — the spirit-king of the earth — Goho Mao-son

And the summary phrase, which is written up around the temple: "All things exist in Sonten."

⇒ This is closer to a cosmology than to a pantheon. The three named figures are not three gods being worshipped side by side; they are three faces the temple assigns to a single energy. ⚑ The third, Goho Mao-son, is the strangest — a being the temple's tradition holds came to this mountain from Venus, and the reason Kurama has a reputation for the occult that its own doctrine does not entirely disown.

Official site (Japanese), teaching page — quoted and translated · checked 2026-08-15

A temple that resigned from its school — and it was not the only one

For most of its history Kurama-dera was a Tendai temple. Then, in 1947, its first modern abbot gathered its accumulated traditions under a new name — Kurama-kokyo — and in 1949 the temple became the head temple of that school.

⇒ 🔴 Hold that date against Thursday. Horyu-ji left the Hosso school in 1950 to found a school centred on Prince Shotoku. Kurama-dera left Tendai in 1949. Two of the oldest temples in the country resigned from their parent schools within twelve months of each other — and it is not a coincidence of temperament. The postwar settlement had just dismantled state control of religion, and the head-temple relationships that the old system had fixed in place were suddenly optional. ⚑ The dates come from each temple's own pages; the explanation is general history and is flagged as such.

Official site (Japanese), history page (1947 and 1949, quoted and translated) · Horyu-ji official site for 1950 · the postwar context is general history, not a temple claim

The boy who trained with the tengu

The temple's history states it plainly: Minamoto no Yoshitsune — as a child, Ushiwakamaru — entered Kurama-dera at about seven years old. He "studied Buddhism by day" at a hall above Yuki Shrine, and "at night, at Sojo-ga-tani, was taught the art of war by the tengu."

⇒ Yoshitsune became the most celebrated battlefield commander in Japanese history and then was hunted to death by his own brother. The story that he learned to fight from mountain demons on this hillside is the founding text of half of Japanese heroic literature, and Sojo-ga-tani is a place on the path you will walk over the ridge — not a metaphor, a location with a signpost.

Official site (Japanese), history page — quoted and translated · checked 2026-08-15

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

You climb it. The temple charges a mountain-entry fee at the gate and the ascent is the visit. Four things:

1. The cable railway is run by the temple, and the temple states it is the only railway in Japan licensed under the Railway Business Act that a religious body operates. A religious body running a licensed railway is a genuine oddity. ⚠ It lands above Yuki Shrine and skips the switchback approach — which is why walking up is the better choice here, not the harder one.

2. The stone mandala in the courtyard outside the main hall. A geometric pattern set into the pavement in front of the Hondo Kondo, with a triangle at its centre where people stand. It marks the point where the temple holds the mountain's energy to be concentrated. ⇒ Whatever you make of that, you will see a queue of people taking turns to stand on a paving stone, which is worth understanding rather than walking past.

3. The treasure hall, two minutes above the main hall — and go early. Its third floor holds a National Treasure Bishamonten triad. ⚠ It keeps shorter hours than the temple and closes on Tuesdays, and it is the reason this day runs front-loaded rather than gently.

4. The roots. On the path over the ridge, at the Kinone-michi, the cedar roots run across the surface of the path rather than under it — the bedrock is too shallow for them to go down, so they lace the ground you walk on. ⇒ It is the single most photographed stretch of the walk and the reason the temple asks you not to press on in the wet or the dark.

Shinto shrine · on the Kurama-dera approach · moved here 940
▶
What it is, and why it is here rather than somewhere better

The tutelary shrine of Kurama village, standing on the switchbacks a few minutes above Kurama-dera's gate — you pass through it on the way up rather than making a trip to it.

It did not start here. It was moved to this mountain in 940 by imperial order, out of the capital. ⇒ ⚑ The reasoning was defensive. Kurama sits due north of Kyoto, and the north was the direction from which misfortune was held to enter a city. Installing a shrine on this slope was a deliberate act of spiritual fortification for the capital below — the same logic that put Enryaku-ji on Mount Hiei to the north-east. A god was posted here, the way a garrison is posted.

The festival is the move, re-enacted every year

The Kurama Fire Festival — one of the three great fire festivals of Japan — is this shrine's annual rite, held on the night of 22 October. Villagers carry enormous burning pine torches up the same approach you are walking.

⇒ It commemorates the 940 procession itself — the night the deity was brought up from the city by torchlight. The festival is not decorative; it is the arrival being performed again, on the actual ground, once a year, for eleven centuries. ⚑ You miss it by ten days.

Kifune Shrine貴船神社
Shinto shrine · Kibune ravine · head shrine of some 2,000 water shrines
▶
What it is

In its own words, "the head shrine of approximately 2,000 water shrines nationwide", enshrining "the water deity, the source of the life of all things." It stands in a cedar ravine at the head of the stream that feeds Kyoto's water. Building work is recorded here from around 686.

Official site (Japanese and English editions) · checked 2026-08-15

Why a shrine to water is a shrine to the state

The founding legend, from the shrine's own English page: about 1,600 years ago Tamayori-hime, mother of the first emperor, declared — quoted directly — "I am Tamayori-hime. E'er I control the rain and the winds to make the land prosper and the soil fertile." She travelled by boat up the rivers and founded the shrine where the water came from.

⇒ In a rice economy, rain is not weather; it is the tax base. A dynasty that cannot deliver water cannot deliver anything, so emperors petitioned this shrine directly, and horses were offered here in droughts and in floods — a black horse to call rain, a white one to stop it. ⚑ That practice is widely held to be the origin of the ema, the small painted wooden tablet hung at every shrine in Japan: a picture of a horse offered when a live horse was not affordable. ⚠ The horse offerings are on the shrine's own pages; the ema derivation is a widely repeated claim that the shrine's English page does not itself state, and is marked here as such.

The other reputation, which is real

Kifune is also, historically, a site of the ushi-no-koku-mairi — the shrine visit at the hour of the ox, 2 a.m., to nail a straw effigy to a sacred tree. The Noh play Kanawa sets it in this ravine, in a story in which a woman comes here to ask to be turned into a demon.

⇒ ⚑ It is the same logic as the rain. A shrine believed to grant petitions with real force is a shrine people bring their worst petitions to. The romantic Kibune of summer dining platforms and the Kibune of the 2 a.m. nail are the same place, and knowing the second makes the ravine read very differently in the dark under the cedars.

Sanjusangen-do (Rengeo-in)蓮華王院 三十三間堂
Buddhist hall · Kyoto · founded 1164 · present building 1266 · 1,001 Kannon
▶
What it is

A wooden hall 120 m long, containing 1,001 statues of Kannon: a thousand life-size standing figures ranked in ten tiers, with one large seated Kannon at the centre. The building and everything in it are National Treasures.

Its name is an architectural measurement. Sanjusan-gen means thirty-three bays — the spaces between the pillars of the inner sanctuary — and thirty-three is the number of forms Kannon is said to take in order to reach different people. ⇒ The building is not a hall that happens to be long. It is a number, built.

Kyoto City official tourism register · English Wikipedia · the trip's own verified record (2026-08-08)

Who built it, and what they wanted from it

It was founded in 1164 at the wish of the retired emperor Go-Shirakawa and paid for by Taira no Kiyomori — the warlord who was in the process of taking over the country.

⇒ ⚑ That pairing is the whole politics of the age in one commission. Go-Shirakawa was an emperor who had abdicated in order to hold power without the ceremonial constraints of the throne. Kiyomori was the first warrior to run the state outright. A retired emperor with authority but no money, and a soldier with money but no legitimacy, jointly built the largest devotional object in the country. Within twenty years the Taira had been annihilated — by Minamoto no Yoshitsune, the boy from Kurama you read about this morning.

Kyoto City official tourism register · English Wikipedia · the trip's own verified record (2026-08-08)

The fire, and what was carried out of it

The hall burned in 1249. Of the thousand standing Kannon, 124 survived — carried out of a burning building — and the remaining 876 were commissioned by the retired emperor Go-Saga and made by three rival sculptural workshops over sixteen years. The present building was completed in 1266.

⇒ So the ranks in front of you are not one set of statues. They are a 12th-century remnant standing among a 13th-century replacement, and the replacement was a national industrial effort with a deadline. ⚑ The central seated Kannon was carved by Tankei, son of Unkei — whose workshop made the guardians at Todai-ji's Great South Gate that you walked under yesterday. Father at Nara, son here, on consecutive days.

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

One long room, walked from one end to the other. It is dim, and photography inside is not permitted. Four things:

1. Look for the older statues among the newer. 124 of the thousand predate the 1249 fire. The 12th-century figures are subtly different in the face and the fall of the drapery from the 13th-century ones beside them. ⇒ You are looking for survivors in a crowd, and the fact that nobody sorted them afterwards is the point.

Shimogamo Shrine賀茂御祖神社（下鴨神社）
Shinto shrine · Kyoto · founding date unknown · World Heritage
▶
What it is

The southern of the two Kamo shrines, at the confluence of the Kamo and Takano rivers. Its formal name means the shrine of the Kamo ancestors, and that is literal: it enshrines Kamo Taketsunumi in the west hall and Tamayorihime in the east — the grandfather and the mother of the god enshrined at Kamigamo, which you reach this afternoon.

Official site (Japanese) · checked 2026-08-15 · ⚠ no English edition: the site's own "English" link leads to a page that was never filled

The shrine says it does not know when it was founded — and its reasoning is better than a date

Its own history page states: "The exact founding of this shrine is unknown, but because there is a record that the sacred fence was repaired in the 7th year of Emperor Sujin, it is thought to have been worshipped from an older age than that."

⇒ ⚑ Read what that argument does. It dates the shrine from a repair. You cannot repair a fence that was never built, so the record of maintenance proves the existence of something older than the record — and how much older is simply unknown. A shrine that declines to give itself a founding year, and instead publishes the logic by which its own antiquity is inferred, is doing something rarer than claiming to be ancient.

The earliest hard documentary proof of its scale is equally sideways: an entry in the 8th-century national chronicle records that in 698 an order was issued to police the Kamo Festival because so many spectators were gathering. ⇒ A crowd-control problem is the first firm evidence that this was already a major institution.

Official site (Japanese), history page, quoted and translated · checked 2026-08-15

How a shrine changes who pays for it

Most shrines do not narrate their own economics. This one does: "over sixty estates throughout the country were donated and supported the shrine. As the Kamakura and Muromachi periods and then the age of wars came on, contact with the estates gradually broke off; in their place the faith of the common people came to support the shrine."

⇒ That is the whole social history of Japanese religious institutions in two sentences, told from the inside. An aristocratic endowment collapses with the aristocracy that granted it; the institution survives by converting itself into something ordinary people fund. Every amulet counter in Japan is downstream of that transition.

Official site (Japanese), history page, quoted and translated · checked 2026-08-15

The rebuilding cycle — and the shrine's admission that it no longer means what it meant

Shimogamo rebuilds on a fixed cycle, established with 1036 as the first — though it notes drily that "owing to wars, famines and disasters it sometimes stretched to thirty or fifty years." The interval is now given as 21 years, and the reason is material: cypress bark and plain wood stay sound a little over twenty years, and the cycle is also what transmits the crafts.

⇒ 🔴 Then comes the honest part, which the shrine states itself: "it is not possible to renew everything. The original rebuilding was a transfer renewing all buildings and furnishings except the sacred object, but the present basic policy is repair of damaged parts."

⚑ Cultural-property designation changed the ritual. Two of the halls are National Treasures and fifty-three more buildings are Important Cultural Properties — and you cannot demolish a National Treasure every twenty-one years, even if that is what the rite requires. The law that protects the buildings has quietly overruled the practice that produced them, and the shrine says so in public. ⇒ Hold this against Kasuga two days ago, which has done its rebuilding sixty times.

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

The precinct is free; a separate paid area holds the old kitchen and the hut. Four things:

1. The seven zodiac shrines beside the main hall — find your own year. They look like one row of small shrines and are actually seven, in three groups, each covering particular birth years across the twelve-animal cycle. ⇒ You are meant to locate yours and pray at that one specifically. Most visitors photograph the row and pray at whichever is nearest.

2. The pond that invented a sweet. Water wells up around the Mitarashi pond, and it is counted among the seven wonders of Kamo. ⚑ The rising bubbles were shaped into dumplings, and that is held to be the origin of mitarashi-dango — a thing sold in every station in Japan, which started at this specific pool of water.

3. The joined tree, on the left of the small shrine near the entrance. Two trunks grown into one, with a young tree sprouting at the root. It is counted among the seven wonders of Kyoto — and the detail worth knowing is that the present tree is the fourth generation, each successor said to be born somewhere in the forest you just walked through.

4. The shrine of seals, right beside the main hall. A small sanctuary for seals — the carved stamps used to sign documents. The shrine's explanation: seals were treated as precious at court and in temples, and "this was gradually deified." ⇒ An office implement, worshipped. It is a genuinely strange thing to find at the heart of a World Heritage site and almost nobody stops at it.

What it is

The northern Kamo shrine, and the one with the strongest English documentation of any place on this trip — its own site publishes a full English edition that is longer and more object-specific than its Japanese pages. It stands on a triangular slope where two streams converge, facing a mountain.

Official site, English edition · checked 2026-08-15

Where the god came from, and why the festival looks the way it does

The myth is the one that started in the forest this morning. Tamayorihime was purifying herself in the river when a vermilion arrow came floating downstream. She took it home, enshrined it, and became pregnant by its power. When her son came of age, his grandfather assembled the gods for a feast and asked the boy to point out his father among the guests. The boy answered that his father was in the heavens — and ascended there himself with a clap of thunder.

His mother prayed to see him once more. He appeared in a dream and gave instructions for the preparations that would bring him back: sacred garments, decorations of aoi and katsura, and a ritual involving horses. When these were carried out he descended, as an adult, onto the mountain behind this shrine.

⇒ 🔴 That is not a decorative legend — it is the festival's charter. The aoi leaves, the katsura branches and the horses of the Aoi Festival are all specified in the god's own instructions. What the shrine calls one of Japan's three imperial festivals — the other two are Kasuga's and Iwashimizu's — is a set of directions from a dream, still being followed.

⚑ And the arrow has an identity, which English retellings usually drop: the older chronicle names it as the thunder deity of a shrine in the neighbouring district. The father is not anonymous.

Yin and yang, and the fact that you can point at it

From the Heian period the Kamo family were noted practitioners of onmyodo, "the way of yin and yang" — used at court to purify, to identify lucky and unlucky directions, and to predict. The shrine states that the philosophy of balance between contrasting forces "is represented in various ways on the shrine grounds."

⇒ 🔴 Three of those objects are in the what-to-look-at list below. A doctrine you can point at is rare; usually you get a doctrine and a signboard.

Every unifier in Japanese history, each with a specific act

The English history reads as a list, and it is worth having as one: Emperor Kanmu made a pilgrimage after moving the capital in 794. Minamoto no Yoritomo bestowed protection. Oda Nobunaga provided a horse for the shrine's horse-race ritual. Toyotomi Hideyoshi sponsored one of the rebuildings. Tokugawa Ieyasu made a pilgrimage in 1600 — the year of Sekigahara. And Emperor Meiji came in 1868 "to inform the shrine deity that governing power in Japan had been returned to the emperor."

⇒ ⚑ Seven centuries of men taking power in Japan, and each of them came here and did something concrete about it. The last entry is the sharpest: a restored emperor formally notifying a god of a change of government.

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

The precinct is free and open; the inner court before the National Treasure halls is a separate guided admission. Five things:

1. Count the pine needles on the two sand cones. Straight ahead as you pass the second torii stand two conical heaps of sand, shaped to represent the sacred mountain. The left cone has three needles set in its tip; the right has two. Odd and even — yang and yin. ⇒ This is the single best instruction anyone can give you at this shrine. The philosophy is not on a plaque; it is a countable difference between two piles of sand, and once you have counted you will not walk past them again.

2. The two guardians are not a matching pair, and that is deliberate. Modern shrines put two identical komainu at the gate. Here it is a komainu on the left, silver, for the moon and yin; and a Chinese lion on the right, gold, for the sun and yang. The shrine notes this combination is the older tradition.

3. Do not cross the arched vermilion bridge. It stands in front of the gate and looks like the way in. It is used only by priests and designated participants during rites — you take one of the bridges beside it. ⚑ A thing you would otherwise get wrong within thirty seconds of arriving.

4. The ema rack at the small shrine across the stream. That sub-shrine is dedicated to the mother of the god in the main hall, and is the most important of the twenty-four subsidiary shrines — the priests at the main sanctuary do not begin their prayers until they hear the call from the priest out here. The mother's rite goes first. ⚑ Its votive tablets carry an image of Murasaki Shikibu and the poem she wrote here, on a pilgrimage to pray for a good marriage: "Oh, songbird! / While I remain here / Waiting for your call / In the forests of Kataoka / My garments grow wet with morning dew."

5. ⚠ The sacred horse will not be there. A live white horse is kept at the shrine and shown to visitors — on Sundays and public holidays only. You are here on a Monday. ⇒ Stated so it is not a small disappointment on the day.

⚑ And it names what made the settlement unusual: up to the Meiji Restoration it was "a settlement of a special character in which the shrine's priests and farmers dwelt together." ⇒ Not a clerical enclave and not a village — both at once, which is why it got its own word.

It is the same water as the shrine

The stream running past these houses is the stream that flows through the shrine precinct. Inside the grounds it is the Nara-no-ogawa; the moment it leaves the precinct it changes its name and runs east past the priests' houses.

⇒ 🔴 And this is the best fact about the place, buried in the city's planning document rather than on any signboard:

"In particular, it is rare that a solidarity is formed through the water — water drawn from the stream into the front-garden ponds is kept pure, and is returned to the same stream."

⚑ Each house takes shrine water into its garden pond and is obliged to return it clean, because the next house downstream takes the same water in. The community's cohesion is not a custom or a sentiment — it is enforced by the direction of flow. A house that fouled its pond would be fouling its neighbour's. ⇒ This is the whole social meaning of the street, and it is a hydraulic mechanism.

What it is

It began as a hut, and the warlords made it

The official account is that it started as a small hermitage which rose because of its founder's personal reputation for austere Zen, then was elevated when two emperors became its patrons and it was styled "the peerless Zen garden of this realm." ⇒ Not a state foundation — a hut that attracted emperors.

It was then laid waste in the Onin War and restored by the monk Ikkyu. And then the sentence that explains everything you will walk past today:

"In the Momoyama period Toyotomi Hideyoshi conducted Oda Nobunaga's funeral here, built Soken-in to pray for Nobunaga's enlightenment, and together with it donated temple lands; taking that as the opening, the Sengoku warlords built sub-temples one after another and it reached its greatest prosperity."

⇒ 🔴 The compound's shape is a consequence of one funeral. Hideyoshi's memorial to Nobunaga opened the door, and the warlords followed him in, each endowing a sub-temple. The lanes you are walking are a Sengoku subscription list.

Official site (Japanese), quoted and translated · checked 2026-08-15

Zen and tea, and the gate that helped kill a man

Daitoku-ji is the institutional home of the connection between Zen and the tea ceremony — all three houses descended from Sen no Rikyu are formal supporters of its anniversary exhibition.

The relationship is also structural. The main gate's first storey was donated by a renga poet who was Ikkyu's lay disciple; the second storey was added by Rikyu himself and named Kinmokaku. And then, in the temple's own careful wording: the story that installing an image of Rikyu up there drew Hideyoshi's anger and became "one cause" of Rikyu's forced suicide is famous.

⇒ ⚑ Note the hedge — "one cause", not "the cause". The temple is more careful than most guidebooks, which turn this into the whole explanation. The offence, in the telling, was that Hideyoshi would have to walk beneath a statue of a commoner tea master to enter.

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

The lanes are free and open; the sub-temples each charge and keep their own days. ⚠ The abbot's quarters, the National Treasure gate and the famous dry garden are in the main compound, which is not normally open to the public at all — and its annual airing of treasures is currently suspended for restoration. 1. Walk the main axis and read it as a plan. From the imperial messenger's gate the buildings run in a straight line north — main gate, buddha hall, dharma hall, abbot's quarters. ⇒ A complete seven-hall Zen monastery laid out on one line is rarer than any individual building in it, and you can pace the whole diagram in a few minutes even with every door shut.

2. Stand under the main gate and think about the second storey. You cannot go up. What it is, and it is a political instrument

Built by Hideyoshi in 1583, the first anniversary of Oda Nobunaga's death, to pray for his enlightenment. Its founding abbot was Kokei Sochin — who was also Sen no Rikyu's own master in Zen.

⇒ 🔴 The operator that runs its openings states the political reading outright, and it is worth quoting because it is unusually blunt for temple copy: this is "a historically most important temple, built so that Hideyoshi could seize the initiative amid the succession struggle after Nobunaga's death."

⚑ Nobunaga was killed in the Honno-ji Incident with no settled succession. Hideyoshi was one of several generals with a claim. Staging the funeral of the dead lord — and building him a temple — is how you make yourself the heir in public. The piety was real and it was also the instrument.

This is a portrait of a specific man made by people who could have known what he looked like.

What it is

Six hundred metres of street climbing the old highway toward Mount Atago, protected as a national preservation district since 1979. Its name means "at the foot of the shrine gate" — the great vermilion torii at the top of the street is the first gate of Atago Shrine, still some way up the mountain.

A village that changed jobs, and you can see where

Kyoto City's own plan gives the sequence: the settlement opened around the end of the Muromachi period, based on farming, forestry and fishing. Then, in the mid-Edo period, as pilgrims to Atago multiplied, it took on the additional character of a temple-gate town, and teahouses appeared among the farmhouses.

⇒ 🔴 And here is why this street is worth walking rather than glancing at: that economic change is legible in the buildings, spatially, and the plan says so outright. The district divides into an upper and a lower half at Adashino Nenbutsu-ji, in the middle. The upper half, nearer the torii, is thatched farmhouse-type buildings. The lower half is townhouse-type — lattice fronts and slatted "insect-cage" windows in the upper storey.

⚑ Walk uphill and the architecture changes from town to countryside at a fixed point. You are reading a village's economic history in the order the buildings were put up — service trade at the bottom near the traffic, farming at the top — and the hinge is a temple full of the dead.

Why anyone was walking up here at all

Atago Shrine, at the top of the mountain, is the shrine of fire suppression — the deity you petition so that your wooden city does not burn. In a capital built almost entirely of timber, that is not a minor cult. Kyoto City describes the district as carrying both the character of a temple-gate town of the fire-preventing deity and that of a farming village.

⇒ ⚑ Every kitchen in Kyoto used to carry a paper charm from this mountain. The traffic that built this street was people walking up to buy fire insurance from a god.

Kiyotaki清滝
Hamlet · Kyoto · the foot of Mount Atago · once a railway resort
▶
What it is

A small village in a river gorge where the pilgrim road reaches the mountain. Kyoto City's own description is brief and exact: "Kiyotaki opened as the post-station of the Atago pilgrimage." It exists because people needed somewhere to sleep before climbing.

Kyoto City tourism register (Japanese), quoted and translated · checked 2026-08-15

Four economies in one hamlet

Read in order, this is one of the more complete small histories you will walk through:

1. A service settlement for mountain ascetics. The village began as the climbing entrance for yamabushi — people who served the practitioners and their lodges settled at the bottom.

2. An Edo post-town. When belief in Atago's warrior-protecting Jizo spread among lords and samurai nationwide, the retainers who escorted daimyo on pilgrimage built houses here, and it filled with inns and teahouses.

3. 🔴 A cutting-edge leisure resort — and this is the part nobody expects. In 1929 a railway opened from Arashiyama to this village, and a funicular ran from here up the mountain. On top there was a hotel, an amusement park with a "flying tower" ride, and a ski ground.

4. Then it stopped. In 1944 the line was abolished as a "non-essential, non-urgent line" — a wartime administrative category — the leisure facilities shut, and the village emptied.

⇒ ⚑ A ski resort with its own railway was deleted by a bureaucratic classification, and the hamlet has been quiet ever since. What you see is not a village that never modernised; it is a village that modernised hard and then had it taken away.

Kyoto City tourism register (Japanese) · Japanese-language reference works on the Atagosan Railway for the chronology · checked 2026-08-15

Basho revised a poem about this place while he was dying

The verse attached to Kiyotaki is:

"Kiyotaki — green pine needles scattering down into the waves."

An earlier version of it read "Kiyotaki — no dust upon the waves; the summer moon." ⇒ Basho revised it on his sickbed in Osaka three days before he died, in 1694, apparently to avoid re-using an image he had just used in another late poem.

⚑ A man with three days to live, adjusting a word in a poem about a river gorge he was not going to see again. ⚠ Two stones carrying the verse are said to stand in the village and downstream, but that rests on informal sources only; look for them rather than expecting them.

Jingo-ji高雄山 神護寺
Shingon temple · Takao · two temples merged in 824 · where Japanese esoteric Buddhism was founded
▶
What it is

A mountain temple reached by a long stone stair up from the river, holding sixteen National Treasures. ⚠ Its website prints, in English, the words "Sorry, Japanese only."

Official site (Japanese) · Takao preservation association · checked 2026-08-15

It is two temples that were merged, and both had a reason

Jingan-ji, "the temple of the deity's vow", was built in 781 by the statesman Wake no Kiyomaro, after he sought an oracle at the Usa Hachiman shrine and was told: "Copy the whole Buddhist canon, make Buddhist images, recite the sutra, build a temple compound, and pray for the peace of ten thousand generations." The temple's name comes straight from that instruction.

At about the same time he built Takaosan-ji here on this mountain. And the temple's own account is careful about what it was for: "rather than a mere clan mortuary temple, it is thought to have been built as a training hall for monks aiming at mountain austerities, dissatisfied with the urban Buddhism of Nara until then."

⇒ 🔴 That sentence explains the entire location. Nara Buddhism, which you spent three days inside last week, had become an institution of the capital — endowed, political, indoors. This place was built by people who wanted to get away from it and go up a mountain. In 824 the two were merged, the name became Jingo-ji, and the whole thing was handed to Kukai.

Official site (Japanese), history pages, quoted and translated · checked 2026-08-15

🔴 Saicho and Kukai both worked here — and in 812 Saicho was initiated by Kukai

Two men founded the schools that would dominate Japanese Buddhism for the next thousand years: Saicho, who founded Tendai, and Kukai, who founded Shingon. Both worked here, in sequence, under the same family's patronage.

In 802 Kiyomaro's sons invited Saicho to lecture on the Lotus Sutra here, as a memorial for their aunt. The temple calls those lectures "the first utterance of Heian Buddhism." On returning from China in 805 he came back and opened Japan's first esoteric initiation altar in this temple. Kukai entered in 809, and in 810 performed here the first state-protection rite he ever conducted.

⇒ 🔴🔴 And then, in 812, the thing that makes this place singular: Saicho received esoteric initiation from Kukai, here. The senior man, already famous, already the founder of a school, knelt to the younger one. Seventeen of Saicho's own disciples followed him a few months later.

⚑ The register of who was initiated survives, in Kukai's own handwriting, and it is a National Treasure. Saicho's name is written at the head of the list. The temple notes something else in it: the name of Taihan — the disciple whose defection from Saicho to Kukai became one of the causes of the rupture between the two men, which was permanent. ⇒ One sheet of paper holds the high point of their collaboration and the seed of its collapse. Because Kukai wrote it as a working memo rather than a document, the temple notes it also shows his free, unconstrained hand.

⚠ It is shown only in early May. You will not see it.

Official site (Japanese), history and treasures pages, quoted and translated · checked 2026-08-15

A monk who wrote a rule against violence into his own temple

The temple was revived in the 12th century by Mongaku, a monk who had strong-armed the retired emperor Go-Shirakawa into funding it. His forty-five article oath of 1185 survives as a National Treasure, carrying Go-Shirakawa's own palm-print in vermilion at head and foot.

Article one: the monks shall be of one mind. Article two: they shall not select by high or low birth. And then the article on protest, which is worth reading twice:

"When there is a grave suit, lead the monks and petition the court… if no judgment is granted, stand in the palace court and spend your whole life doing so. Even if you die without returning to the temple, you shall never, by private force, plan battle and settle it by victory or defeat, nor flatter officials and give bribes."

⇒ ⚑ This is written in the century when armed monks from the great temples were a standing threat to the capital. Mongaku, who had personally coerced an emperor, then made permanent law forbidding his own successors to use force or bribery — and required them instead to stand and wait until they died. Go-Shirakawa, the man he had coerced, put his hand on it in red.

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

Up a long stone stair from the river; the precinct is spread over terraces. Five things:

1. 🔴 The Daishi-do, and you are inside its one week of the year. Most of this mountain has burned repeatedly — and in the Onin-era fires the Daishi-do was the only building left standing. It is a reconstruction of Kukai's own living quarters, and it is built like a house rather than a hall: four bays on its left flank and five on its right, an asymmetry no ritual building would have. ⚑ Inside is a plank-carved image of Kukai, carved in 1302 as a copy of an older image in Shikoku — and the temple notes that the image it was copied from can no longer be identified. ⇒ It is opened 1–7 November, and you are there on the 3rd. A plank-carved figure is rare in Japan, and this is one of the few days it is visible.

2. The main hall is 1935, and its patron is worth knowing. The vermilion Kondo, the pagoda above it and several other buildings were all donated in the 1930s by a single lay devotee. ⇒ Most of what looks ancient here is ninety years old — the antiquity of this temple is in its objects and its story, not its timber.

3. The Yakushi in the main hall (National Treasure) — look at the face. The temple's own description is unusually vivid: "a piercing, fixed gaze; a thick nose-ridge and fleshy nostrils; lips boldly thrust forward and clamped." And its explanation: "such an uncanny physiognomy, which raises dread in the worshipper, may be a product of the presence of ascetic mountain practitioners." ⇒ The statue looks severe because the men who commissioned it were.

4. Throw the clay discs at the western end. From the garden at the far west of the precinct, above the gorge, visitors throw small unglazed earthenware discs out into the ravine — to carry misfortune away with them. ⚠ The claim that this temple invented the practice circulates widely but rests on informal sources; the practice itself and its purpose are properly documented.

5. Something that started here and left. In 939, during a rebellion, a Fudo image carved by Kukai was carried from this temple east to subdue it — and afterwards the image "would not move", and a temple was founded around it where it stood. ⇒ That temple is Narita-san, today one of the most visited in Japan. It began as this mountain's statue.

What it is

The middle temple of the three, small and quiet, reached over a vermilion bridge and up two flights of maple-covered steps. Founded in the 824–834 period by Chisen, a senior disciple of Kukai — as a branch temple of Jingo-ji.

Official site (Japanese) · checked 2026-08-15 · no English edition

It was founded to do a different job from the temple up the road

Jingo-ji was where the esoteric transmission happened. Saimyo-ji was opened as a kairitsu dojo — a training hall for the monastic precepts, the rules governing how a monk lives. Its own site still frames it that way: "a mountain temple that still values the quiet environment young monks need for practice."

⇒ ⚑ So the valley was organised by function. One temple for the doctrine and the initiations, another twenty minutes down the road for the discipline. That is a monastic complex spread across a landscape rather than a compound, and walking between the two is walking between departments.

It stayed a dependency for 466 years. In 1290 a retired emperor granted it its own temple title, and it became independent of Jingo-ji. It burned in the wars of the 1560s and was restored in 1602 by a vinaya master of the early-Edo precepts revival — the same function, four centuries later. ⚑ Its nine-article monastic rule from that restoration still hangs as a wooden tablet in the guest hall.

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

Small; half an hour is enough. Five things, and the first is on the way in:

1. The stone pillar on the left of the steps. Carved with four characters meaning "the outer aspect of the great boundary" — a marker declaring that above this point the ground is pure. ⇒ You cross a line rather than arrive at a gate.

2. The main hall's plan is wrong for its sect, and that is the point. Built in 1700, donated by the mother of the fifth Tokugawa shogun, entirely of zelkova. Seven bays by four, divided into three along its depth — the centre the inner sanctuary, the flanking bays serving as the outer. The temple states plainly that this is "an unusual plan for the main hall of a Shingon temple." ⇒ Having just seen orthodox halls all week, this is the one that does not conform, and you can see it from the doorway.

Kozan-ji栂尾山 高山寺
Shingon-lineage temple · Toganoo · refounded 1206 by Myoe · World Heritage

What it is

The topmost of the three temples, in cedar woods, with one surviving medieval building and an archive of over twelve thousand items. ⚑ Its own English site notes that everyone pronounces it "Kozanji" and that the official pronunciation is "Kosanji."

Its name comes from a plaque granted by a retired emperor in 1206 reading "the temple of the high mountain the rising sun first illumines" — a phrase from the Avatamsaka Sutra, the text this temple was refounded to study.

Official site, English edition and Japanese edition · checked 2026-08-15

The man this place is about

Myoe (1173–1232) is one of the strangest figures in Japanese religious history, and the temple does not tone him down.

⚑ He lost both parents at eight and was tonsured under Mongaku — at Jingo-ji, the first temple you visited today. ⇒ Today's last temple was founded by a man the day's first temple raised, and he went back and rebuilt Jingo-ji as an adult.

The temple's own biography, in three sentences: "In his youth he cut off his right ear out of the will to seek the Way, and out of longing for Shakyamuni twice planned a journey to India." He never went. He kept his calculated itinerary — the daily distances, the arrival dates — in a sutra bag for the rest of his life, with a note in the margin reading "Ah, ah — how I long to go."

He cut the ear off in front of a particular painting, which survives here as a National Treasure and carries his own handwriting on it, including the words "the honoured mother of the earless monk" and a poem: "Think of me together with pity, O Buddha — there is none who knows me but you." ⇒ Orphaned at eight, he took Shakyamuni as his father and this painted figure as his mother, and wrote it on the painting.

Official site (Japanese), biography page written by a named Kyoto University academic, quoted and translated · checked 2026-08-15

His philosophy, and the fact that he staged his own death to demonstrate it

Myoe's teaching is called gonmitsu — his own fusion of Kegon doctrine with Shingon esoteric practice, two systems normally kept apart.

⇒ 🔴 And the temple makes an extraordinary claim about how he died: he arranged the room — placing a Maitreya image, hanging a specific mandala, chanting two particular mantras — and the temple's own verdict is that "the scene of his death was precisely gonmitsu made concrete." The Kegon objects and the esoteric objects, side by side, around a dying man who had spent his life arguing they belonged together.

His maxim, which the temple leads with and which survives on a wooden tablet: aru beki yo wa — the temple's own English is "do what is appropriate," glossed as ask yourself how you should behave given the time and the circumstances, and act accordingly. ⚑ Seven syllables, no metaphysics, from the man who cut off his ear.

The animal scrolls — and where they actually are

This temple owns the Choju-jinbutsu-giga, four scrolls of rabbits, frogs and monkeys behaving like people, universally described as the ancestor of Japanese comics.

⚠ 🔴 You will not see them here. The originals are on deposit at the national museums in Tokyo and Kyoto — ownership remains with this temple, but the objects are elsewhere. The temple's own English page says, of the most famous scroll, that "a reproduction of this scroll is displayed at Sekisuiin." ⇒ What you see here is a copy, and the temple says so.

What the temple does say about them is worth carrying: the artist is unknown. The traditional attribution is doubted, other names have been proposed, and its own page states flatly: "None is confirmed; the artist is unknown." They are thought to continue a tradition of impromptu caricature drawn by monks.

The tea claim, and the temple's own deflation of it

Kozan-ji is "known as the place where tea was first made in Japan." The account is that Eisai brought seeds from China and gave them to Myoe, who planted them here and recommended tea to the monks because it dispelled the sleep that hinders practice. Tea from here was later called honcha, "true tea", and all other tea hicha, "not tea." Cultivation spread from here to Uji.

⇒ ⚑ And then the temple undercuts its own monument, which is why this is worth quoting rather than repeating. There is a stone here reading "the oldest tea garden in Japan" — but the temple's own page states that the oldest garden was actually across the river, and that the stele stands on the site of a former monastic hall instead. The temple tells you its own landmark is in the wrong place.

Wooded, steep in places, quiet. Four things:

Wednesday 4 November — Daigo-ji and the climb to Kami-Daigo

South-east Kyoto · a garden a dying man designed for one party, the oldest wooden building in the prefecture, and a mountain that is the original temple
Sanbo-in三宝院
Head priests' residence · Daigo-ji · founded 1115 · garden laid out by Hideyoshi in 1598
▶
What it is

Not a temple hall but a residence — the house the head priests of Daigo-ji lived in. The temple's own page dates it exactly: "Sanboin temple was built in 1115 by Shokaku, the 14th archbishop of Daigoji Temple." Almost every building in it is an Important Cultural Property; two are National Treasures — the main drawing room, the Omote-shoin, and the gate, the Kara-mon. Daigo-ji's English site calls the Omote-shoin "one of the most iconic buildings of Medieval Japan."

Daigo-ji official site, English grounds page and English Sanboin page; Japanese Sanboin page for the 1115 date · checked 2026-08-15

The garden was designed by a dying man for a single afternoon

What you look at from the drawing room is not an old temple garden that grew. It was drawn by Toyotomi Hideyoshi himself in 1598 — the temple's Japanese page says he "personally made the basic design" — and it was made for one event: the flower-viewing party held here on the fifteenth day of the third month of 1598.

The scale of that party is recorded on the temple's own archive pages. Seven hundred cherry trees were dug up and transplanted along the approach — brought in from the home provinces around the capital and from Yoshino. The abbot of Sanbo-in, Gien, hosted it and wrote it up in his diary, calling it "a great rejoicing for the temple" and "a satisfaction of my own person." Hideyoshi's wife, his consort Yodo, his son Hideyori and Maeda Toshiie were all there.

The temple is named after a taste

This is the strangest fact about the place and it is on the temple's own pages. Daigo is a Buddhist word for the last and finest stage of refining milk — the clarified essence — used as a metaphor for the highest teaching. The temple is named for it because of what happened at a spring on the mountain above.

The temple's own founding account: in 874 the monk Shobo, known posthumously as Rigen Daishi, was led by a manifestation of the local deity Yokoo Daimyojin to a sacred spring, and built a small hall there housing two Kannon images — Juntei and Nyoirin. The temple's archive feature adds the scene: he had been praying for a place to establish esoteric teaching and saw five-coloured cloud rising to the south-east of the capital; on the summit, by the water, an old white-haired man was drinking. He said the water had the taste of daigo — and vanished.

⇒ ⚑ The spring got the name, the temple took the spring's name, and the word underneath all of it means "the best thing you have ever tasted." The spring is still running, and you can drink from it — it is at the top of today's climb, not down here.

Two National Treasures, and one of them was brought here from another province

The Kondo, the main hall, was first raised by decree of Emperor Daigo in 926. But the building standing there now is not from here. ⇒ ⚑ The centrepiece of the lower precinct is a transplant, exactly like the seven hundred cherry trees. Hideyoshi's restoration of this temple was carried out by importing whole objects — a hall from one province, an orchard from two others.

The five-storey pagoda is the older survivor and the reason the lower precinct matters historically. Emperor Suzaku ordered it in 936 as a memorial to his father, Emperor Daigo; it was finished in 951, under Emperor Murakami. It stands about 38 metres, of which roughly 13 metres is the metal spire. It is the oldest wooden building in Kyoto Prefecture — older than anything in the city centre, because the city centre kept burning and this did not.

What it is — and it is a beginning, not a branch

Kinpusen-ji is the head temple of its own school, and it claims to be the place Shugendo — Japanese mountain asceticism — started. The founder is En no Gyoja, and the temple's founding account is a specific event rather than a general reverence.

After opening sacred mountains across the country, he came into the Yoshino and Omine ranges and finally undertook a thousand days of unbroken practice on the summit of Sanjogatake. He asked for a form of the Buddha that could save people living in real suffering. First Shakyamuni, the Thousand-Armed Kannon and Miroku appeared, in their gentle forms. He judged that those forms could not reach violent and desperate people, and kept praying.

🔴 Then, in the temple's own words: heaven and earth roared, the great bedrock of the summit split apart, and with the thunder there welled up a buddha in a form of raging wrath. That is Zao Gongen — the three gentle buddhas having thrown away their gentleness and come back angry.

The temple explains the colour and the anger together: the whole body is blue-black, and that blackness is said to represent compassion and tolerance. The word it uses is jo — the anger that forgives everything anyway. Its own comparison: the anger of a loving parent.

▶
🔴 It was built because people thought the world had just ended

This is the fact the whole place hangs on, and the temple states it directly. In 1052 the regent Fujiwara no Yorimichi took a villa from his father Michinaga and turned it into a temple. The temple's own English page on why:

"In Buddhism, there is the Mappo 'end-of-the-world' theory and it was believed that the year 1052 marked the first year of the beginning of the end-of-the-world. This theory captured the heart of many aristocrats and monks, and, as a result, people became more devout in Buddhism and believed in the Buddhist Pure Land."

Mappo is the doctrine that the Buddha's teaching decays in stages, and that in the final stage no one can achieve enlightenment by their own effort any more. 1052 was the year the calendar said that stage began. The hall went up the following year.

⇒ 🔴 You are not looking at a temple built out of piety. You are looking at what the richest man in Japan built in the first year of what he believed was the end of the world — a scale model of the paradise he now had no other way of reaching.

⇒ 🔴 What you are looking at from outside is a shelter. The National Treasure is the three small buildings inside it — one per deity, the middle one, for the prince, deliberately the smallest. The shrine's own site does not explain the sizing; the arrangement is described by the prefecture and the national record, and the reason is not stated by either.

The worship hall in front is a separate National Treasure and about a century younger — its cypress was felled in the early Kamakura period, against the main hall's late Heian timber. It is built in a residential idiom rather than a religious one.

⚠ On the famous dating figures: tree-ring dates in the 1050s–1060s for the main hall circulate widely. The claim traces to a named study by the Nara research institute and the Uji city museum, but I could not find it published on either institution's own pages, so what is stated above is what the national and prefectural records themselves say: late Heian timber, early twelfth century by construction detail. Both readings agree it is the oldest; they differ on how precisely it can be dated.

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

Small, quiet, and quick — it is the counterweight to Byodo-in rather than a rival to it. Five things:

1. Look past the outer hall to the three shrines inside it. Find the two large ones and the small one between them. ⇒ That middle building is the prince's, and it is the smallest of the three.

2. The roofs, on both buildings. Cypress bark, laid in courses — nagare-zukuri, where one slope of the roof runs down long over the front. ⇒ This is the form nearly every Japanese shrine uses, and this is the oldest one left.

3. The worship hall in front, and the fact that it looks domestic. Early Kamakura, in an aristocrat's residential idiom. ⇒ It is a house form doing religious work — the same borrowing you saw in the drawing room at Sanbo-in two days ago, five centuries earlier.

Manpuku-ji黄檗山 萬福寺
Head temple of the Obaku school · Uji · founded 1661 by a Chinese monk · built Chinese and still is
▶
🔴 It is a Chinese temple, and that is not a figure of speech

There is a temple called Wanfu-si on Mount Huangbo in Fuzhou, Fujian. Its abbot, Yinyuan Longqi — Ingen in Japanese — came to Japan in 1654, aged 63, with about twenty disciples. He was granted land here, and in 1661 he founded a temple which he gave the same name as the one he had left, written with the same characters: Obaku-san Manpuku-ji.

He never went home. The temple's English page: "Instead of returning to China, he decided to found Obaku-san Manpuku-ji and ended up making Japan his permanent home."

The complex was begun in 1661 and the national record dates its provisional completion to 1679 — eighteen years of building.

What makes it look wrong, and why that is the point

The temple's own account of its buildings: they are "different from ordinary Japanese temple architecture," built in the style of late-Ming China, the country Ingen came from. The national record puts it more precisely — the layout and the halls show the influence of Chinese Buddhist architecture from the end of the Ming into the early Qing, and are valuable in the history of early-modern building for exactly that reason.

🔴 And it is not even made of Japanese wood. The temple states the construction timber is teak, native to South and Southeast Asia — the main hall being, in its words, the only and largest historic building in Japan made of teak. Its English page names the teak as Sumatran; the Japanese page says only South and Southeast Asian. Both are the temple's own, and the broader statement is the one used here.

Four details the temple singles out as things you will not see at other Japanese temples: balustrades in a manji-kuzushi broken-swastika pattern · round windows · arched Obaku ceilings · and peach-shaped ornaments on the doors, a charm against evil. Its own summary: no other temple in Japan preserves its founding-era appearance this completely.

🔴 They still chant in Chinese

This is the detail that turns the building from a curiosity into a living thing. The temple: "In the Obaku school, the liturgy and the sutras are all faithfully inherited as they were performed in China." The reading used is toon — Chinese-based pronunciation.

The temple gives its own worked example. The opening of the Heart Sutra, which in an ordinary Japanese temple is chanted "Maka hannya haramita shingyo…", is chanted here as "Poze poromi to shinkin…"

⇒ ⚑ If you hear a service, you are hearing a Chinese liturgy that has been maintained unbroken in Japan for over three hundred and sixty years by people who mostly do not speak Chinese. The chanting is also sung rather than intoned — a four-beat form with bells, drums and gongs, which the temple describes as being performed like music.

Zen has three schools in Japan, and this one was not allowed to be one until 1876

The temple's own history: it was originally called something like "the Obaku branch of the Rinzai school", and because of shogunate policy it stayed that way. Only in 1876 did it become independent and take the name Obaku-shu. Its own sentence: Zen in Japan is classified into three schools — Rinzai, Soto and Obaku.

⇒ 🔴 For more than two hundred years after it was founded, the third school of Japanese Zen was formally a sub-branch of the first. Independence came from a Meiji reorganisation, not from a doctrinal victory.

⚑ And this puts the day in a longer sequence. Zen came to Japan from China in the thirteenth century, brought by Chinese monks, and by the time Ingen landed it had been naturalised for four hundred years. ⇒ He arrived as a foreign teacher into a country that already had what he was bringing — and the visible difference is that this time the buildings stayed Chinese. The temple notes that its abbots were Chinese monks for thirteen successive generations.

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

A large, flat, symmetrical compound on a central axis, with matched halls left and right. Six things:

1. The paving underfoot, and the rule attached to it. A path of diamond-shaped stones runs through the precinct, cut in a pattern the temple says is modelled on dragon scales — and because a Zen monk of great power is likened to a dragon, only the abbot may walk on the lozenge stones. ⇒ A rule almost nobody notices, stated openly on the temple's own page.

2. The kaipan at the dining hall — a wooden fish. The temple describes it as a fish-shaped ritual implement that announces the hours of the monastery's daily observances. Next to it hangs a bronze plate struck at the morning and midday meals. ⇒ This is not an ornament. It is the clock, and it has been telling this compound what time it is since the seventeenth century.

3. In the main hall, count the rakan. There are eighteen, not the sixteen a Japanese temple would have — the temple says this matches the arrangement in Ming halls in China. ⇒ The count itself is the evidence of where the building came from.

4. The doors and windows of that hall. Peach-carved door panels against evil; round windows either side. ⇒ Both are Chinese vocabulary, and neither belongs to Japanese temple building.

5. The Tenno-den, the first hall you pass through. It holds the Four Heavenly Kings, Idaten, and a seated Hotei carved by a Chinese sculptor in 1663 — and here Hotei is not a folk luck figure but Miroku, the buddha of the future, himself. ⇒ The fat laughing statue you have seen in shop windows all trip is, in this building, a buddha.

6. The founder's hall, reached by a path of irregular stones. The temple describes the paving as an ice-crack pattern, no two stones the same shape. Inside is a portrait statue of Ingen made in 1663 while he was alive, for his seventieth birthday, with real hair and beard set into it. ⇒ It is the only object on the grounds made from a living face.

Shukkei-en縮景園
Daimyo garden · Hiroshima · begun 1620 · 1,370 m from the hypocentre
▶
What it is

A lord's garden begun in 1620 by Asano Nagaakira, first Asano lord of Hiroshima, and designed by his chief retainer Ueda Soko — a noted tea master. It reached its present shape after a long rebuilding between the 1780s and 1830s, and in 1804 thirty-four individual views within it were formally named. It marked its four hundredth year in 2020.

The name is usually rendered "shrunken scenery." The garden's own account traces it to a preface by the Confucian scholar Hayashi Razan, containing a line about compressing sea and mountains into this ground and gathering the scenery into this pavilion. A second tradition holds it was built as a miniature of West Lake in China. ⚠ The garden gives both and settles neither.

Shukkei-en official site, introduction and history pages (Japanese), translated · checked 2026-08-15

6 August 1945, in the garden's own words

The garden's history page states it plainly, and it is worth reading as written rather than summarised:

"Many precious lives were lost in the atomic bombing of 6 August 1945. Numerous buildings within the garden, beginning with the Seifukan, were reduced to ruins in an instant, and great ancient trees were burned; the appearance of former days was entirely lost. However, the pond that forms the garden's foundation — Takuei Pond — along with the artificial hills and the Kokokyo bridge, though severely damaged, retained their former form."

The garden is 1,370 metres from the hypocentre, a distance the city has surveyed precisely. Restoration began in 1949; it reopened, unfinished, in April 1951, and the full work took roughly thirty years.

⚠ One thing the garden's own page does not describe, and a government tourism text does: a memorial to atomic-bomb victims stands near the river here, built after photographs came to light showing that bodies had been buried in makeshift graves in the garden after the attack. That account is not on the garden's own site; treat it as reported rather than confirmed, and look for the memorial's own inscription when you are there.

What was here before

The park occupies the head of the delta where the Ota River divides into the Motoyasu. The city's own account of what stood on it: the Nakajima district, which "benefited from river transport and the Saigoku Kaido passing through it, and had been one of Hiroshima's leading entertainment districts since early modern times." The same page continues: "the atomic bombing instantly annihilated the area, close as it was to the hypocentre, reducing it to scorched earth."

⇒ This is not open ground that was landscaped. It is a commercial quarter that was destroyed, and then not rebuilt.

The law, and why it was legally unusual

The park exists because of the Hiroshima Peace Memorial City Construction Law, promulgated on 6 August 1949. It passed both houses of the Diet unanimously in May — and then required something almost no Japanese law does.

🔴 Because it was a special law applying to a single local government, the constitution required a local referendum. It was promulgated after receiving over ninety per cent support from Hiroshima's voters. ⇒ ⚑ The city voted on whether to become a peace memorial city, and that vote is the legal foundation of the ground you are standing on.

Hiroshima Peace Memorial Museum official English history timeline, quoted verbatim · checked 2026-08-15

The competition, and Tange's axis

The design was chosen by open competition, announced in April 1949. By the deadline of 20 July, 145 proposals had been submitted. The winner was the group led by Kenzo Tange, then at the University of Tokyo. Construction ran 1950 to 1955; the park opened to the public on 1 April 1956. It covers 122,100 square metres and holds thirty-five memorial monuments.

🔴 The single most important thing to know before you walk in is that the park is a sightline. The city's own words: it works to preserve "the north-south axial viewscape from beneath the Main Building of the Peace Memorial Museum, across the Cenotaph for the A-bomb Victims, to the Atomic Bomb Dome." The city's history page calls it, in the phrase that stuck, "Tange's axis."

⇒ 🔴 And it is protected by law. Since 4 January 2022 the city has enforced a landscape and building-height framework so that, from that fixed vantage point, no buildings are visible behind the Dome. ⚑ A view is a legal object here.

⚑ One further design fact: the railings of the Peace Bridge and West Peace Bridge, which frame the park's southern entrance, were designed by the sculptor Isamu Noguchi.

The Cenotaph, its inscription, and the question about it

The Cenotaph was built on 6 August 1952, designed by Kenzo Tange. Its shape is drawn from an ancient haniwa clay house — the city explains the choice as coming from a wish to shelter the dead from rain and dew.

The inscription, in the city's own published English: "Let all the souls here rest in peace; For we shall not repeat the evil."

The stone chest beneath holds the Register of the Names of the Fallen — the city's foundation notes it records the dead regardless of nationality, with names added when a relative applies. 🔴 At the dedication on 6 August 2026 it held 353,639 names in 132 volumes, plus a further volume for the unidentified. ⇒ ⚑ Eighty-one years on, the register is still growing; it held 297,684 names in 108 volumes in 2015.

⚠ The inscription has been argued over since it was set, because it does not say who "we" are. The city's own answer, in its published FAQ, is that it is a vow by all people — carrying what it calls the heart of Hiroshima, which endures past sorrow, overcomes hatred, and prays for the coexistence of all humankind. The city has installed explanatory panels in eight languages in front of the Cenotaph specifically to convey that reading. The historical dispute over the wording is real and long-running, but the city's own pages address the meaning without naming the episode, so only the city's position is stated here.

Atomic Bomb Dome原爆ドーム
Ruin · Hiroshima · built 1915 · World Heritage 1996 · Special Historic Site 2025
▶
What the building was

It was an exhibition hall. Construction began in 1914 and it was completed in 1915 as the Hiroshima Prefectural Products Exhibition Hall, designed by the Czech architect Jan Letzel — brick, three storeys, with a five-storey stairwell at the centre carrying an elliptical dome. It was renamed in 1921, and again in 1933 as the Hiroshima Prefectural Industrial Promotion Hall.

It sold and displayed the prefecture's products and held art exhibitions and trade fairs. From 1944 it housed wartime government offices.

City of Hiroshima official pages on the Dome and its Special Historic Site designation (Japanese), translated · checked 2026-08-15

What happened, and why the frame is still there

At 8:15 on the morning of 6 August 1945, the bomb detonated approximately 600 metres above the centre of the city. The hall stood approximately 160 metres from the point below the detonation. It was gutted by blast and heat, caught fire from the ceiling downward, and burned out. Everyone inside — staff of the civil engineering office and several timber companies — died instantly.

The city's own explanation of why the skeleton survived when the district did not: the blast struck it almost vertically, from nearly overhead, rather than from the side. ⇒ It stands because it was closest, not despite it.

⚠ On the numbers, quoted with the city's own qualifications and not rounded here. The city states the population at the time was approximately 350,000 — residents, military personnel, people mobilised from surrounding towns to demolish buildings, and people from Japan's colonies, some of them conscripted labourers. On the dead: "The exact number of deaths from the atomic bombing is still unknown. Estimates place the number of dead by the end of December 1945, when the acute effects of radiation poisoning had largely subsided, at roughly 140,000." And: roughly 50 per cent of those within 1.2 kilometres of the hypocentre are estimated to have died that day; approaching the hypocentre, 80 to 100 per cent died.

🔴 It was nearly demolished, and a schoolgirl's diary changed that

Preservation was not the obvious course. Many in the city wanted the ruin taken down as an unbearable reminder. The turn is credited by the city to the diary of Kajiyama Hiroko, who was exposed to the bomb at the age of one and died of leukaemia fifteen years later. Her diary read:

"Only that pitiful Industrial Promotion Hall will, forever, keep appealing to future generations about the dreadfulness of the atomic bomb."

The city began a structural survey in 1965; the city council resolved to preserve the building in July 1966; fundraising and the first preservation works followed in 1967. ⇒ There have been five preservation campaigns in all — 1967, 1989–90, 2002–03, 2015–16 and 2020–21 — and the city re-surveys the structure roughly every three years. Each campaign used the minimum reinforcement needed to keep the ruin in the condition the bomb left it in.

World Heritage, December 1996. Getting there took a change in Japanese law. When the city petitioned in 1992, the government demurred: nomination required domestic legal protection first, and the historic-site rules then only reached to the mid-Meiji era — the Dome was too recent to qualify. 🆕 And on 18 September 2025 it became a national Special Historic Site — Japan's highest tier of historic-site protection. What it is, and the crow that chose the site

The shrine dates itself to 593, the year Empress Suiko came to the throne. Its own founding account is specific: a divine command came to Saeki no Kuramoto, who governed the island; led by a sacred crow the goddess had brought from the Plain of High Heaven, he circled the island's bays with the deities and chose the place where the seawater ebbs and flows to build the shrine.

Taira no Kiyomori built the present shrine in 1168 — in shinden-zukuri, which the shrine's own English page describes as "an architectural style in which residences of the nobility were built in those days." ⇒ ⚑ A courtier's mansion form, put up on posts in the sea, for a god. Retired emperors came: Go-Shirakawa in 1174, Takakura twice in 1180, bringing the culture of Kyoto with them.

⚠ One very common explanation is NOT in the shrine's own words, and it is worth knowing that. What it is

Begun in 1629 by Tokugawa Yorifusa, founding lord of the Mito branch, as the garden of his Edo residence, and completed under his son Tokugawa Mitsukuni. Its own page calls it the oldest surviving daimyo garden of Edo, and notes that its form — a stroll garden circling a pond — became the model that later daimyo gardens followed.

Koraku — "pleasure afterwards" — is the second half of that sentence. The garden's own page adds that Mitsukuni made this his political creed.

⇒ ⚑ A Tokugawa lord named his private pleasure garden after a maxim instructing rulers to take their pleasure last, and had it explained to him by a refugee from the empire the Manchus had just destroyed.

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

A compact circuit, an hour or so, with the city visible over the trees. Six things:

1. The Full Moon Bridge. A stone arch said to have been designed by Zhu Shunshui himself, invited as an honoured guest-teacher. Its reflection completes a circle on the water, which is where the name comes from; the garden calls it among the oldest of its type outside Kyushu and Okinawa. ⇒ Stand where the reflection closes the arc.

2. The great pond, and what it is pretending to be. The central "sea," carrying an island of the immortals — and the garden states that Shogun Tokugawa Iemitsu himself is said to have drawn the shape of the pond, made to evoke Lake Biwa.

3. The West Lake causeway. A reproduction of the Su Causeway at West Lake in China — and the garden claims it as the first depiction of that causeway in any Japanese garden. ⇒ ⚑ Two water features quoting two different lakes, one Japanese and one Chinese, in one garden.

4. The Oi River stretch, with its two bridges. A reproduction of a river in Kyoto that Iemitsu liked; he is said to have sat on a rock in it directing the work. The bridges are named Tsutenkyo and Togetsukyo — both borrowed from real Kyoto originals. ⇒ You have been near the real Togetsukyo, at Arashiyama, on 3 November.

5. The rice paddy — and it is still farmed. The garden says Mitsukuni had it made to teach the hardship of farmers and the value of agriculture to the wife of his heir. Local schoolchildren still plant and harvest it every year. ⇒ A working teaching device, three and a half centuries on.

6. Tokujin-do, the small hall — the only original building left. Mitsukuni read the chapter on Boyi in Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian at eighteen, admired the two brothers who starved rather than serve an unjust ruler, and enshrined their statues here. Its name comes from a phrase in the Analects: seeking benevolence, and attaining benevolence. ⚠ The garden states this is the only building retaining its original construction-era appearance — the formal Chinese gate you enter through burned in the war and its reconstruction was completed at the end of 2020.

What is inside, at full scale

The permanent exhibition works by building things at their real size rather than describing them.

Nihonbashi. You enter the Edo zone by walking across the bridge. Kiyosumi Garden清澄庭園
Garden · Fukagawa · rebuilt from 1878 by the founder of Mitsubishi · a garden made of imported rocks
▶
What it is

A pond-and-woodland stroll garden. The technique is an Edo daimyo one, but the garden's own page makes a specific claim about this example: the method carried on into Meiji-era garden design and reached its modern culmination here.

The ground was, by tradition, partly the estate of the great Edo merchant Kinokuniya Bunzaemon; in the 1716–36 period it became the lower residence of the lord of Sekiyado, and the garden took rough shape then.

🔴 Then in 1878 Iwasaki Yataro — the founder of Mitsubishi — bought the derelict property, and made a garden of it for his employees' recreation and for entertaining distinguished guests. It was substantially finished in 1880 under the name "Fukagawa Friendship Garden." Work continued after his death: the great pond was dug and fed with water drawn from the Sumida River.

Tokyo Metropolitan Park Association official page for Kiyosumi (Japanese), translated · checked 2026-08-15

🔴 The rocks came by ship, and that is the whole point of the place

Kiyosumi is famous for meiseki — named stones. The garden's own page lists eight representative kinds by their place of origin: Izu shore stone · Iyo blue stone · Kishu blue stone · Ikoma stone · Shikinejima stone · Sado red-jewel stone · Bitchu granite · Sanuki granite. These are quarries scattered across the whole country.

🔴 And here is the mechanism, in the garden's own sentence: the Iwasaki family gathered them from stone-producing regions nationwide using their own company's steamships.

⇒ ⚑ A shipping company built a garden out of the thing a shipping company is uniquely able to move. The garden adds that, counting paving, bridges and stepping stones, the number of stones is uncountable and the place takes on the character of a stone garden. ⚠ No total is published; do not expect a number.

Tokyo Metropolitan Park Association official page (Japanese), the eight stone types and the steamship sentence quoted and translated · checked 2026-08-15

🔴 In 1923 it saved lives, and that is why you can walk in it

The garden's own account of the Great Kanto Earthquake, and it is the reason the place is public:

"Kiyosumi Garden suffered great damage in the Great Kanto Earthquake, but at that time it unexpectedly served as a refuge in the disaster and saved many lives. The Iwasaki family, placing weight on this disaster-prevention function that gardens possess, donated the eastern half — the part less damaged, which is the present garden — to the City of Tokyo as parkland the following year, 1924. The city developed it and opened it to the public in July 1932."

⇒ 🔴 A private garden became a public one because it had already been used as one, in an emergency, without permission. ⚑ The owners drew the correct conclusion and gave it away.

The Tudor-style brick house that stood here, designed by the British architect Josiah Conder, burned in the same earthquake. The garden itself was designated a Tokyo Metropolitan Place of Scenic Beauty on 31 March 1979 — ⚠ a metropolitan designation, a clear step below Koishikawa Korakuen's national double designation this morning. Same city, same managing body, two different tiers of protection.

Tokyo Metropolitan Park Association official page (Japanese), the earthquake passage quoted verbatim and translated, and the 1979 designation; the Conder house from the Association's own English page · checked 2026-08-15

Visiting it — and what to actually look at

Small, flat, and close to the water. Four things:

1. The isowatari — the stepping stones across the pond edge. The garden states the design intent outright: stones set at intervals so that the view changes with every step you take. ⇒ Walk them slowly; that is the instruction the garden gives itself.

2. Pick three stones and read their labels. Izu, Iyo, Sado — three different coasts. ⇒ You are standing in a map of Japan's quarries, assembled by a shipping line.

3. The Ryotei, the tea-style hall out over the water. Built by the Iwasaki family to receive Field Marshal Kitchener, who came as a state guest in 1909. ⚠ It survived the earthquake and the war but was completely rebuilt in 1985, and was named a Tokyo Selected Historic Building in 2005. ⇒ Old function, new fabric — worth knowing before you assume.

4. The Basho stone. The garden confirms a monument here carved with his most famous verse — the old pond, the frog jumping in, the sound of water. ⚑ It was not always here: the Association's English page says it stood on the bank of the Sumida and was moved into the garden when the embankment works were carried out. ⇒ Even the poem about a pond was imported, like the rocks.
